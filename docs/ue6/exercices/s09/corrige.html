Note pédagogique : le texte oppose deux idées de l'efficacité. Pour la directrice générale, elle vient de l'outil : une seule base de données, des réparations suivies en temps réel, moins de saisie, un coût informatique prévisible (l. 16-27). Pour l'autrice, elle dépend de l'organisation : un système d'information, ce sont aussi des personnes, des procédures et des données, et les grands projets d'ERP dérapent souvent (l. 28-32). Les deux voix critiques déplacent la question vers la distance : distance avec les locataires exclus du numérique (l. 33-39), distance entre les équipes de bureau en hybride et les agents de terrain suivis par leurs tablettes (l. 40-46). Le risque cyber montre que le nuage partage le risque sans le supprimer (l. 47-55). La question oblige à relier ces plans : le numérique ne rend le bailleur plus efficace que s'il garde un canal humain pour ceux qui en ont besoin. Aucune connaissance technique n'est attendue (JUR-T05) : ni architecture ou paramétrage de l'ERP (UE 5), ni procédure de sécurité, ni article de loi, ni droit du logement ; aucun calcul ; aucun nom d'éditeur ou d'hébergeur réel.

2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres se regroupent en quatre idées au lieu d'être récités : les réparations progressent mais une partie des locataires reste insatisfaite ; une part notable de la population vit hors ligne ou a très peu de compétences numériques ; le travail hybride dépend fortement du diplôme ; les attaques sont fréquentes et peu d'organisations vérifient leurs fournisseurs.

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "One database, two workforces? An East Midlands landlord takes its tenants to the cloud", was published in The Midland Shires Enterprise Journal, a (fictitious) British business publication, on 13 November 2026. Written by Rhiannon Ashworth-Bello, it uses the case of a fictitious housing association near Nottingham and Derby, Brindleholme Housing, to ask whether replacing old, separate systems with cloud software, a tenant app and tablets makes a social landlord more efficient, and at what human cost.

Main idea — The chief executive argues that a single information system will make repairs faster, cut paperwork and free time for the most vulnerable tenants (l. 16-27). The author shows that this efficiency is not automatic: it depends on people, procedures and data (l. 28-32), and it raises three risks: excluding tenants who are offline, dividing the workforce, and exposing sensitive data (l. 33-55). She ends on the need to keep a human contact (l. 60-62).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A revealing failure — A tenant had to explain the same boiler leak three times, and the plumber knew nothing about her previous calls or her son's asthma (l. 1-4).
  2. The landlord and its old systems — Brindleholme is a non-profit housing association registered with the regulator, with some 650 staff, almost half in the field, and about 18,000 homes; its work was spread across a dozen pieces of legacy software that "did not talk to each other" and hand-kept spreadsheets (l. 5-10).
  3. Pressure from tenants and the regulator — Large landlords must report tenant satisfaction measures: 83 per cent of non-emergency repairs were on time in 2025/26, against 77 per cent two years earlier, but about one in six tenants (16 per cent) are still dissatisfied with their landlord's overall service (l. 11-15).
  4. The chief executive's answer — An ERP and a housing management system rented in the cloud; a tenant portal and mobile app for repairs and rent; tablets that update jobs in real time; automatic reminders; one database for everyone; hybrid working for office teams; a monthly subscription instead of servers, so that IT costs become predictable; every hour saved on paperwork goes to the tenants "who need us most" (l. 16-27).
  5. A first warning — Technology alone will not deliver efficiency: an information system is also people, procedures and data; wrong records spread faster; large ERP projects often overrun because training and procedures are underestimated, and Brindleholme's own launch has already slipped (l. 28-32).
  6. The tenants' objection — The chair of the tenants' committee fears digital exclusion: elderly tenants and those who cannot afford broadband risk becoming "a ticket number" (l. 33-36); a government plan cited estimates that 1.6 million people in the UK live offline and that a quarter of the population have the lowest level of digital skills; tenants want the promise of a phone line in writing (l. 36-39).
  7. The union's objection — The tablets record where operatives are all day ("My members feel watched"), and hybrid work suits office staff but not field staff (l. 40-43); nationally, 28 per cent of working adults were hybrid workers in early 2025, but 41 per cent of graduates against 4 per cent of people with no qualifications (l. 43-46).
  8. The cyber risk — The database holds tenants' incomes, health and vulnerabilities; 43 per cent of UK businesses and 28 per cent of charities identified a breach or attack in a year, phishing being by far the most common, yet only 15 per cent of businesses reviewed their direct suppliers' cyber risks; the cloud shares the risk, but the landlord remains responsible, a duty that the 2025 data law did not change (l. 47-55).
  9. A European trend and its energy cost — 52.7 per cent of EU businesses paid for cloud computing services in 2025; large data centres may be more energy-efficient than an old server room, but their electricity demand keeps rising (l. 56-59).
  10. An open conclusion — For the chief executive, digital tools should give staff more time with vulnerable tenants; for the tenants' chair, the test is whether "a real person" will still answer (l. 60-62).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Having summarised the article, I will now turn to the question: does going digital make a social landlord more efficient, or more distant from the people it serves? I will first show what digital tools can genuinely improve, then explain why they may create distance with tenants and between staff, before arguing that efficiency and closeness can go together if the human channel is kept."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). « More efficient » invite à demander pour qui et selon quelle mesure (délais de réparation, coût, satisfaction) ; « more distant » invite à distinguer la distance physique (moins de guichets, moins d'appels) et la distance relationnelle (ne plus être écouté) ; « the people it serves » désigne d'abord les locataires, mais un bon candidat y inclut les salariés de terrain. Le statut d'organisme sans but lucratif compte : l'efficacité n'y sert pas un profit mais une mission.

Plan A — More efficient, as long as it stays human

  • Problematic: Can a social landlord use digital tools to save time without losing contact with its tenants?
  • I. Real gains: one database instead of a dozen systems (l. 8-10, 21-22); repairs followed in real time, fewer repeated calls (l. 1-4, 20-23); predictable IT costs through a subscription (l. 24-26); a regulator that measures repair times (l. 11-15).
  • II. Real risks of distance: digital exclusion of elderly and low-income tenants (l. 33-38); location tracking of field staff and a two-tier workforce (l. 40-46); a "ticket number" culture (l. 36).
  • III. Conditions for success: keep the phone line and face-to-face contact, in writing (l. 39); train staff and redesign procedures (l. 28-32); involve tenants and unions in the project; use the time saved for vulnerable tenants (l. 26-27, 60-61).
  • Conclusion: going digital makes a social landlord more efficient only if it is a choice offered to tenants, not imposed on them.

Plan B — By stakeholder

  • I. Tenants: convenience for those who are online (reporting a repair at night, paying rent by app, l. 18-20), but exclusion for those who are not (l. 33-38); trust in how sensitive data is protected (l. 47-48).
  • II. Staff: office teams gain flexibility through hybrid working (l. 23-24), field staff gain information on tablets (l. 20-21) but may feel watched (l. 41-42); the divide between those who can work from home and those who cannot (l. 43-46).
  • III. The organisation and society: cost control and a mission-driven use of savings (l. 25-27); dependence on a cloud provider and cyber risk (l. 47-55); the environmental cost of data centres (l. 57-59); a wider European move to the cloud (l. 56-57).

Plan C — Technology, people, governance

  • I. Technology is necessary but not sufficient: an information system is more than software (l. 28-29); data quality comes first (l. 29-30); large ERP projects often overrun (l. 30-32).
  • II. People make or break the project: digital skills of tenants and staff (l. 37-38), training, trust, the risk of surveillance (l. 41-42).
  • III. Governance: choosing and controlling the supplier, keeping responsibility for tenants' data (l. 52-55), measuring results with tenants rather than only with software (tenant satisfaction measures, l. 11-15), deciding which services stay human; the candidate can compare with what they know of French social landlords, without unsourced figures.

Useful notions from the programme — 2.1.a information system vs information technology, people, procedures, data ; 2.1.b legacy software, ERP, database, roll-out, data quality, project overrun ; 2.1.c cloud computing, software as a service, subscription, tenant portal, mobile app, supplier dependence, data centre ; 2.2.b hybrid working, field staff, tablets, location tracking, two-tier workforce ; 2.2.c efficiency, automation, real time, paperless, productivity ; 2.3.a cyber security, phishing, data breach, supplier risk (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les chiffres du régulateur, de l'ONS et de l'enquête cyber sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the cloud is dangerous », « the tenants' chair is right »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : parler seulement de la cybersécurité, ou seulement du travail hybride, sans discuter l'efficacité et la distance avec les locataires.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : réciter des définitions de l'ERP ou du nuage sans lien avec la question.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : architecture du système, modèle de données, paramétrage de l'ERP, procédures de sécurité, articles du droit des données.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« one point six millions », « eighteen thousands », « fifty per cent » pour fifteen).
  • Propre au sujet : confondre information system et IT (l. 28-29) ; croire que les 83 % concernent toutes les réparations (il s'agit des réparations non urgentes, l. 13) ; lire les 41 % comme une part de tous les travailleurs (il s'agit des diplômés du supérieur, l. 45) ; dire que le nuage supprime le risque cyber (le texte dit qu'il le partage, l. 53-54) ; inventer des chiffres sur le coût ou les gains de l'ERP ; nommer un éditeur, un hébergeur ou un bailleur réel comme fautif ; prendre Brindleholme Housing ou The Midland Shires Enterprise Journal pour des entités réelles.
