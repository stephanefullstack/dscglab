Three clocks, one company? A Denver engineering firm calls its staff back

A new boss wants engineers at their desks and "merit" instead of diversity programs. In Mexico and India, colleagues ask where that leaves them.

By Brynn Halloway-Starke (fictitious author)

It is eight in the morning in Denver when the weekly review of a bridge design begins. In Monterrey, project manager Ximena Cantú Arreola has been at work for an hour; in Pune, structural engineer Vaishali Sathaye joins from home after dinner. Few of them have ever met in person. 5 Talvenridge Engineering, a fictitious firm owned by its partners, designs bridges, water and sewer networks and roads, mostly for cities and state agencies. It employs about eighteen hundred people. It has bought design offices in Monterrey and Pune, which now do much of its design work. Its project teams span three time zones, and most members work remotely for part 10 of the week. Its new chief executive, Garrett Lindqvist-Shaw, recruited from a large industrial group, wants to "make us one company again." From January, American staff will be expected in the office four days a week, and the Denver headquarters will become a flex office with shared desks. The 15 diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) program will give way to a "merit-based" policy: the same rules for hiring, pay and promotion, with no targets for any group. Remote work remains common. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, 35 percent of employed Americans did some or all of their work at home on days 20 they worked in 2025. In October 2026, 22.1 percent of people at work teleworked, and 35.3 percent in architecture and engineering jobs. He is not alone: many large employers have issued return-to-office mandates. DEI has also become a legal question. On January 21, 2025, a presidential executive order revoked a 1965 order on affirmative action by federal 25 contractors and required contractors to certify that they run no DEI programs that break federal anti-discrimination law. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) warns that a DEI initiative may be unlawful if race, sex or another protected trait motivates an employment decision. The administration says it is restoring fairness; critics fear that useful 30 programs are being dropped. Many of Talvenridge's public clients spend federal money. Mr. Lindqvist-Shaw says the plan is about engineering, not politics. "Young engineers learn by sitting next to experienced ones, by overhearing a call about a crack in a pier. You cannot learn that on a video call." When a site 35 has a problem, he wants a team that can meet within the hour. And "everyone should be judged on the same criteria." In Monterrey, Ms. Cantú Arreola hears another message. "None of us has ever had a desk in Denver. Are we a second-tier subcontractor?" Meetings are always set at Denver time. Then there is feedback: "In Denver, a manager 40 says 'this drawing is wrong' in front of everyone and calls it efficient. Many of my engineers would rather hear it one to one." Yet she refuses to generalize: "I am Mexican, but an engineer first, and some of my team love blunt feedback." In her view, the diversity program's mentoring and wider recruiting helped the firm hire and keep rare water specialists. 45 In Pune, Ms. Sathaye worries about isolation. A young engineer may not question a senior colleague's figures on a call, she says, and Denver reads the silence as agreement. Two colleagues left this year for local firms where "you are not always the last to know." For her, talent retention starts with inclusion and training: managers of scattered teams should learn 50 to listen, to give feedback each person can hear and to read cultural signals without stereotypes, which researchers call cultural intelligence. She also wants better use of collaboration tools: written decisions, recorded meetings and late calls that rotate between the three offices. There are three models. Keeping everyone in the office builds a common 55 culture and helps beginners, but leaves the teams abroad outside and adds car commutes. Organized hybrid work, with fixed team days, keeps contact while protecting time to focus. Fully remote work, with a week together every quarter, treats all three offices alike, but asks managers to build trust and to judge results rather than presence. 60 Distance does weaken informal learning, and time zones are a real constraint. A merit-based policy is not unfair by nature, and DEI programs have critics of their own. But "one company" will sound hollow in Monterrey and Pune unless Denver changes its habits too. A team that rarely shares an office can still become one team, if its managers learn to lead it.

Question

Can a company build one team out of people who rarely share the same office, country or culture?
