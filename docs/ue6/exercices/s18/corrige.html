Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Stop the line or stock the shelf? An Oregon wheelchair maker rethinks quality", was published in The Willamette & Cascade Business Lantern, a (fictitious) Pacific Northwest business publication, on November 24, 2026. Written by Annika Sorensby-Vale, it tells the story of Talvenridge Mobility, a fictitious employee-owned maker of power wheelchairs in Albany, Oregon, which faces more complaints and returns, and asks whether it should chase zero defects or rebuild its safety stocks, while it gives quality to self-managed teams.

Main idea — Faced with defective chairs, the company is split between a head of quality who wants to build quality at the source and a head of purchasing who wants safety stocks to protect patients from late suppliers; at the same time, it removes a layer of management and hands quality to its shop-floor teams (l. 14-43). The author concludes that quality is a strategic choice rather than a cost of inspection, and that building it at the source, with the people who make the product, costs less than defects, returns and recalls (l. 58-60).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. The case — Talvenridge has about six hundred employees, is privately held and has been employee-owned since its founder retired; complaints and returns have risen, mostly because of electronic controls and batteries (l. 1-6).
  2. Why quality is a matter of safety — The FDA treats a power wheelchair as a medical device in the middle of its three risk classes, and classifies and publishes recalls, the most serious of which involve a risk of serious harm or death; a defective chair can leave a person stuck at home (l. 7-13).
  3. The case for zero defects — The head of quality wants total quality management: each team checks its own work at the source, anyone can stop the line at the first defect, and suppliers are fewer and monitored more closely; a fault costs more the later it is found, and a recall can ruin the company's name; stock hides problems; in 1987, Congress already said that poor quality could cost up to 20 percent of sales and saw worker involvement as a source of better quality (l. 14-24).
  4. The case for safety stock — The head of purchasing and logistics shares the goal, but battery and electronics lead times can double; without a stock of controllers and finished chairs, a late shipment means a patient waits weeks; zero defects also slows deliveries and raises prices; yet too much stock is no answer either, since batteries age and some were scrapped; nationally, manufacturers' inventories-to-sales ratio fell from 1.56 to 1.46 months of sales in a year (l. 25-35).
  5. A flatter organization — Shift supervisors are gone and self-managed teams own the quality of their work; for a technician who is also a shareholder, this means more pride and responsibility, but also more pressure and slow decisions when nobody is clearly in charge (l. 36-43).
  6. The user's view — A wheelchair user wants a chair that is safe, delivered on time and repaired quickly; Colorado has required makers to give owners and independent repairers access to parts, tools and manuals since 2023; Talvenridge now repairs, refurbishes and lends returned chairs instead of scrapping them (l. 44-51).
  7. Nuances — Zero defects is a direction, not a destination; a reasonable safety stock protects patients, a large one hides faults and becomes waste; self-managed teams need clear roles and training; share ownership motivates but does not replace organization; insurers and public programs do not always pay for quality (l. 52-57).
  8. Conclusion — Quality is a strategic choice: built at the source, with the people who make the product, it costs less than what it prevents (l. 58-60).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Having summarized the article, I will now turn to the question: should manufacturers aim for zero defects, even if it costs more and takes longer? I will first show why aiming for zero defects makes sense, then look at its costs and limits, before arguing that zero defects is best seen as a direction that people, suppliers and stocks must serve together."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06) ; les arguments du texte ne sont pas répétés sans analyse (JUR-C05). Le candidat gagne à distinguer qui paie le coût et quand : le « coût » du zéro défaut se paie avant la livraison (formation, arrêts de ligne, meilleurs fournisseurs), celui des défauts après (réparations, retours, rappels, réputation, et l'attente du patient). Il peut aussi noter que « takes longer » joue dans les deux sens : arrêter la ligne ralentit la fabrication, mais un fauteuil en panne fait attendre le patient bien plus longtemps. La question vaut pour tous les fabricants : l'exigence n'est pas la même pour un fauteuil, un frein de voiture ou un tee-shirt. Il évite tout calcul.

Plan A — Zero defects: a direction worth its cost

  • Problematic: Is zero defects a realistic target, or a costly illusion?
  • I. Why manufacturers should aim for zero defects. The cost of a defect grows at each step (l. 18-20); recalls and reputation (l. 9-12, 20); safety and independence of users (l. 12-13, 44-46); quality as a strategic choice, already argued in 1987 (l. 21-24).
  • II. The costs and limits. Slower deliveries and higher prices (l. 31); unstable suppliers (l. 26-27); stock-outs that leave patients waiting (l. 28-30); payers who do not pay for quality (l. 56-57); zero is never fully reached (l. 52).
  • III. Zero defects as a horizon. Quality at the source plus a reasonable safety stock (l. 53-54); fewer suppliers, monitored more closely (l. 17-18); trained teams with clear roles (l. 54-55); continuous improvement instead of a slogan.
  • Conclusion: manufacturers should aim for zero defects as a direction, and decide case by case how much time and money each step deserves, starting with what can harm users.

Plan B — Who should be in charge of quality?

  • I. Quality by inspection in a traditional hierarchy: end-of-line checks, rework, alerts that travel up the chain (l. 15-16, 36-37).
  • II. Quality built by the people who make the product: self-managed teams, the right to stop the line, employee-owners who feel responsible (l. 16-17, 38-41); worker involvement already seen in 1987 as a source of better quality (l. 23-24).
  • III. The limits of a flat organization: pressure, slow decisions, unclear roles (l. 41-43); share ownership is not organization (l. 55-56); managers still needed to arbitrate, train and deal with suppliers.

Plan C — The cost-quality-time triangle

  • I. Quality versus cost: prevention costs less than failure (l. 18-20, 22-23), but the price rises and payers resist (l. 31, 56-57).
  • II. Quality versus time: stopping the line and choosing fewer suppliers slow production (l. 31); just-in-time against just-in-case (l. 27-30, 33-35).
  • III. Quality versus the planet: scrapped batteries and wasted stock (l. 32-33, 53-54), repair and refurbishment (l. 47-51): fewer defects also mean less waste.

Useful notions from the programme — 6.2.a quality control, inspection, quality at the source, defect, rework, zero defects, total quality management (TQM), cost of quality, cost of poor quality, product recall, continuous improvement, cost-quality-time trade-off ; 6.2.b inventory, stock, safety stock, buffer stock, stock-out, warehouse, finished goods, components, obsolete stock ; 6.2.c just-in-time, just-in-case, lean, resilience ; 6.1.b supplier, lead time, supply chain disruption, dual sourcing, supplier relationship ; 5.2.a hierarchy, layer of management, delayering, flat organization, self-managed team, empowerment, accountability ; 6.2.e scrap, waste, repair, refurbish, right to repair, circular economy (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite la FDA, la loi de 1987, le ratio du Census Bureau et la loi du Colorado sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the head of quality is right », « safety stocks are a waste »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : décrire la qualité totale ou la gestion des stocks en général sans dire s'il faut viser le zéro défaut malgré son coût et son délai.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : réciter des méthodes de qualité, des auteurs ou des modèles de gestion des stocks sans lien avec le cas.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : chiffrer un coût de la non-qualité, dimensionner un stock de sécurité ou un point de commande, citer des méthodes statistiques de contrôle ou un référentiel de certification par son numéro, détailler le droit des dispositifs médicaux, donner des conseils de santé.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« one comma forty-six », « six hundreds employees », « twenty percents », « nineteen hundred eighty-seven » au lieu de « nineteen eighty-seven »).
  • Propre au sujet : croire que la responsable des achats rejette la qualité (elle partage l'objectif mais craint la méthode, l. 25-26, et admet qu'un gros stock n'est pas la solution, l. 31-33) ; croire que le zéro défaut supprime tout stock (le texte défend un stock de sécurité raisonnable, l. 52-54) ; croire que la FDA classe les fauteuils dans la catégorie de risque la plus élevée (classe intermédiaire, l. 9) ; croire que le ratio de 1,46 est un pourcentage (ce sont des mois de ventes, l. 34) ; croire que les fabricants américains ont réduit leurs stocks (le ratio baisse, mais les stocks ont augmenté en valeur ; ce sont les ventes qui ont progressé plus vite, l. 33-35) ; croire que le Congrès a créé en 1987 une norme obligatoire (c'est un prix, l. 21-22) ; confondre defect nom (/ˈdiːfekt/) et to defect (faire défection) ; parler des utilisateurs de fauteuil comme de personnes « souffrant de » ou « cloués » dans leur fauteuil (préférer wheelchair users, people who use a wheelchair) ; donner des conseils médicaux ; nommer une entreprise, une marque ou un fournisseur réels, ou accuser une entreprise réelle pour un rappel récent ; prendre Talvenridge Mobility ou The Willamette & Cascade Business Lantern pour des entités réelles.
