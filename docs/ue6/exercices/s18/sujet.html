Stop the line or stock the shelf? An Oregon wheelchair maker rethinks quality

Faulty chairs keep coming back. An employee-owned company weighs zero defects against safety stocks, and hands quality to its shop-floor teams.

By Annika Sorensby-Vale (fictitious author)

Talvenridge Mobility, a fictitious company, builds power wheelchairs and mobility aids in Albany, Oregon, in the Willamette Valley. It has about six hundred employees and is privately held. Since its founder retired, it has been employee-owned: a retirement plan holds the shares for the staff. Last year, 5 complaints from patients rose and more chairs came back to the factory, most of them with faulty electronic controls or batteries. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) treats a power wheelchair as a medical device, a battery-operated machine that gives mobility to people limited to a seated position, and places it in the middle of its three risk classes. It also 10 classifies recalls and posts them in a public database; the most serious class covers products with a reasonable chance of causing serious health problems or death. A defective chair is more than an unhappy customer: it can leave a person stuck at home, unable to get to work or to the doctor. Rafael Ostrander-Kim, the head of quality, wants total quality management and 15 zero defects. "We used to inspect chairs at the end of the line and send the bad ones back for rework," he says. "Now each team checks its own work at the source, and anyone can stop the line at the first defect." He also wants fewer suppliers, monitored more closely. A fault fixed on the bench costs a few minutes; fixed in a patient's living room, it costs a technician's day; a 20 product recall can cost the company its name. "Stock hides problems," he adds. "When spare parts are everywhere, nobody asks why a part failed." When Congress created a national quality award in August 1987, it declared that poor quality could cost companies as much as 20 percent of their sales, and it linked worker involvement to better quality. 25 Bettina Marchetti-Lowe, who runs purchasing and logistics, shares the goal but fears the method. The lead times of battery and electronics suppliers can double from one month to the next. "We used to keep a safety stock of controllers and a few finished chairs in the warehouse, just in case," she says. "If a battery shipment is late and the shelf is empty, a patient waits weeks for a chair. A 30 stock-out here is not a lost sale. It is a person who cannot leave the house." Zero defects, she warns, also means slower deliveries and pricier chairs. Yet a big inventory is no answer either: batteries age on the shelf, and last spring pallets of batteries stored too long were scrapped. Nationally, according to the Census Bureau, manufacturers held inventories worth 1.46 months of sales in 35 September 2026, against 1.56 a year earlier. The company has also removed a layer of management. In the old hierarchy, a technician who spotted a problem told a supervisor, who told a manager. The shift supervisors are gone: self-managed teams now plan their own work and own the quality of what they build. Wendell Achterberg-Ruiz, an electronics 40 technician, likes the change. "We own part of this company, so a bad chair is our problem," he says. "I am prouder of my work." But the flat organization has a cost. "There is more pressure now. And when a decision involves two teams, it can drag on for days, because nobody is clearly in charge." Imogen Castellano-Reyes, an accountant who uses a power wheelchair, is blunt. "I 45 don't care how the factory is organized," she says. "I need a chair that is safe, that arrives on time, and that someone can repair quickly when it breaks." Since January 2023, a Colorado law signed in June 2022 has required power wheelchair makers to give owners and independent repairers access to parts, tools and repair manuals. Talvenridge now repairs and refurbishes returned 50 chairs and lends them to users waiting for a new chair, instead of scrapping them. Zero defects is a direction, not a place a factory ever reaches. A reasonable safety stock protects patients from unreliable suppliers; a large one hides faults and ends up as waste. Self-managed teams only work if roles stay clear 55 and people are trained. Owning shares motivates workers, but it does not replace organization. And quality has a price that insurers and public programs do not always want to pay. Still, quality is a strategic choice, not a cost of inspection. Built at the source, with the people who make the product, it costs less than the defects, 60 returns and recalls it prevents.

Question

Should manufacturers aim for zero defects, even if it costs more and takes longer?
