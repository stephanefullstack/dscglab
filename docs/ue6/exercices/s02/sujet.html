Built in Ohio, checked in Brussels: the new reality of workplace AI

American companies have embraced generative AI faster than their rulebooks. Now European law is reaching across the Atlantic, and Brussels has just bought them some time.

By Dana Whitcombe (fictitious author)

When the engineers at Corvane Systems, a fictitious payroll software company in Columbus, Ohio, first tested a generative AI assistant, nobody in the room mentioned Brussels. The tool drafted replies to customer questions, summarized contracts and flagged unusual expense claims in seconds. Two years 5 later, the same assistant comes with a compliance file thicker than its user manual, because about a third of Corvane's clients are based in Europe. The company is part of a broader trend. According to the US Census Bureau, from 17 to 20 percent of American businesses reported using AI between December 2025 and May 2026, and the figure reached 37 percent among firms 10 with 250 or more employees. In the European Union, Eurostat found that almost 20 percent of enterprises used AI in 2025, while 32.7 percent of people aged 16 to 74 had used generative AI tools, and 15.1 percent for work. For American firms, the question is no longer whether to use these tools but under which rules. The EU's Artificial Intelligence Act entered into force 15 on August 1, 2024. A short list of banned practices, such as social scoring, and a duty to promote "AI literacy" among staff have applied since February 2025. Many of the remaining rules became applicable on August 2, 2026, including transparency duties: people must be told when they are talking to a chatbot. Crucially, the law follows the product, not the passport. It covers companies 20 based outside the EU when their systems are sold in Europe or when their output is used there. For a software vendor in Ohio, that is not a detail. Then Brussels hesitated. Under a digital simplification package proposed in November 2025 and in force since July 2026, the strictest obligations, for "high-risk" uses such as hiring, credit scoring or education, were pushed back 25 to December 2027, and to August 2028 for AI built into products like toys or elevators. Supporters called it common sense; critics called it a retreat. Corvane's managers have drawn their own conclusion. Rather than maintain one product for Europe and another for the rest of the world, they now apply the European rules everywhere. "Our customers in Texas ask the same questions as 30 our customers in Dublin," says Marcus Telford, the company's fictitious chief operating officer. "Who checked the model, and what happens when it is wrong?" The approach has a familiar ring. When the EU's data protection rules took effect, many global companies found it simpler to extend them to all their users. Lawyers call this the "Brussels effect": a large market exports its 35 standards because companies prefer one rulebook to several. Not everyone is convinced the pattern will repeat. In Washington, the wind is blowing the other way. An executive order signed on December 11, 2025, called for a single national framework for AI and ordered a task force to challenge state laws seen as obstacles to innovation. For many American executives, 40 the lesson is that rules can be negotiated, delayed or overturned. "Every postponement is read in America as proof that the rules are optional," says Ingrid Solvang, a fictitious compliance advisor based in Dublin. Small firms have another worry: cost. Documenting how an algorithm screens job applicants requires lawyers, auditors and engineers that a startup may 45 not have. Some fear that strict European rules will mainly benefit the largest technology groups, which can afford entire compliance departments. There is also a question the debate in Brussels rarely puts first: energy. Data centers used about 4.4 percent of US electricity in 2023, and a report for the Department of Energy expects that share to reach between 6.7 and 50 12 percent by 2028. Corporate buyers increasingly want to know not only whether an AI tool is safe, but also how much power it consumes. The delay gives American companies breathing room, not an exemption. Those that use it to map their AI tools, train their staff and document their choices will find the European deadlines easier to meet. Those that wait may 55 discover that their biggest customers in Europe have stopped waiting.

Question

Should American companies treat the EU's AI rules as a global standard to adopt rather than a foreign constraint to minimize?
