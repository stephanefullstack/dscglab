Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

  1. la synthèse du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
  2. le commentaire organisé du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un entretien en anglais de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

Le document est un article de presse fictif rédigé pour l'entraînement à partir de faits vérifiés ; la publication, le titre, l'autrice, l'établissement de monnaie électronique, l'université et les personnes citées sont fictifs ; les banques sanctionnées en 2014, les concurrents et les émetteurs de jetons stables ne sont pas nommés.

Sending pounds home: whose money should a Leicester app trust?

A remittance app weighs a pound-backed stablecoin against the banks as regulators redraw the rules of money.

By Imogen Ashworth-Lyle (fictitious author)

Tellanby, a fictitious e-money firm in Leicester with about a hundred and twenty staff, lets thousands of people send money through its app to relatives in India, Kenya and Nigeria. Its two founders, both children of immigrants, watched their parents queue at transfer counters. Its board, including a venture capital investor, 5 must choose: settle transfers with a stablecoin, wait for a public digital pound, or stay with the banks. People in low- and middle-income countries received about $704 billion in remittances in 2025; India alone received some $151 billion, more than any other country. Yet sending money remains costly, a barrier to financial inclusion. The 10 United Nations wants the cost of remittances cut below 3 per cent of the sum sent by 2030, with no route above 5 per cent. In the third quarter of 2025, sending $200 still cost 6.36 per cent on average worldwide, according to the World Bank, which tracks these flows and prices. Only Bank of England notes and Royal Mint coins are legal tender in England and 15 Wales, yet they make up about 3 per cent of all money, says the Bank. Most of the rest is bank deposits. When customers top up the app, their pounds become e-money. Unlike deposits, it is not covered by the scheme that compensates savers when a bank fails; the firm must keep customers' funds separate, under rules the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) tightened in May 2026. 20 Most deposits come from lending: as the Bank explained in 2014, commercial banks create new money whenever they grant a loan. The central bank steers this money supply through its interest rate. On 5 November 2026, it raised Bank Rate by a quarter of a point to 4 per cent. Inflation reached 3.6 per cent in October, and the Bank expects about 5 per cent by mid-2027. 25 Meera Dabhi-Walsh, Tellanby's co-founder and chief executive, wants a stablecoin: a digital token issued by a private firm and backed one for one by safe assets in pounds. "Families see a small fee, then lose far more through a hidden mark-up on the exchange rate," she says. "A pound-backed token, settled in minutes with every charge shown upfront, would give that money back." 30 In November 2014, the FCA fined five banks a total of £1.1 billion after their traders tried to manipulate benchmark exchange rates. "Customers remember the rigging," Ms Dabhi-Walsh says. In July 2025, a United States law set rules for dollar stablecoins fully backed by cash and Treasury bills. "If Britain is too slow," she warns, "the business will go to dollar coins." 35 Fiona Castellane, a fictitious former central banker at the fictitious Kenwick University, is wary. "A stablecoin is only as safe as its reserves," she argues. If households moved large sums out of deposits, banks would lend less to homebuyers and small firms, and the central bank would have a weaker grip on money and credit. Hence the Bank of England's draft rules for large sterling stablecoins, set out in 40 June 2026: at least 30 per cent of the backing held as deposits at the central bank, the rest in short-term government debt, and a cap of £40 billion per coin for now. The Bank and the Treasury are also designing a digital pound, a central bank digital currency that would work like digital cash, with a decision on next steps due in 2026 and no launch before the second half of the decade. Critics fear for privacy 45 and for banks; the Bank says it would not see how people spend, and a digital pound would avoid the energy-hungry methods of some cryptocurrencies. For Esther Nwankwo-Biddle, a fictitious nurse who sends money to her mother in Enugu, the exchange rate matters more than the technology. Nigeria's naira has suffered a sharp depreciation: World Bank data show that a dollar bought about 426 50 naira on average in 2022 and more than 1,500 in 2025, while consumer prices rose by 33.2 per cent in 2024. "A weak currency means my pounds buy more naira," she says, "but prices at home keep rising. And a token in pounds still has to become naira at the other end." Kenya's shilling, by contrast, has regained part of the value it lost against the dollar in 2023. 55 Some directors say no new money is needed: show the full cost, mark-up included, and let competition do the rest. "New money can make sending help home cheaper," says Ms Dabhi-Walsh. "But people trust money because someone stands behind it."

Question

Should the money we use every day be issued by private companies, or remain in the hands of banks and central banks?
