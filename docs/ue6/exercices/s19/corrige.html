Note pédagogique : le texte suit une association qui, pour pourvoir ses postes vacants, fait deux choix discutables et en paie le prix en confiance. Le premier est publicitaire : une campagne choc, placée sans autorisation autour d'un grand tournoi (l. 12-29). Elle réussit (partages, candidatures) mais suscite des plaintes, le rappel des droits des sponsors et l'objection d'une enseignante en publicité (l. 20-38). Le second relève des ressources humaines : un logiciel trie l'afflux de candidatures, vite et avec les mêmes critères pour tous, mais écarte sans explication des parcours atypiques (l. 39-48), ce que les constats de l'ICO rendent plausible (l. 49-54). Le contexte chiffré (l. 5-11) explique la pénurie, et la conclusion (l. 55-59) rappelle que ni une campagne ni un logiciel ne remplacent de meilleurs salaires et de meilleurs horaires. La question porte sur le premier choix (choquer, profiter d'un grand événement) ; le second revient dans l'entretien (questions 5 à 7). Aucune connaissance technique n'est attendue (JUR-T05) : ni analyse juridique du droit de la publicité, des marques, des données personnelles ou de la non-discrimination, ni technique publicitaire chiffrée, ni fonctionnement des algorithmes de tri ; le tournoi est fictif, et le candidat n'a pas à nommer de sponsors, de campagnes choc réelles ni d'éditeurs de logiciels.

2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres se regroupent en une idée au lieu d'être récités : le secteur manque de bras parce que les postes sont mal payés et précaires, que le recrutement à l'étranger est fermé et que la population vieillit.

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Seen by thousands, sorted by software: a Belfast care charity's recruitment gamble", was published in The Lagan & Foyle Business Beacon, a (fictitious) Northern Irish business publication, on 24 November 2026. Written by Orla Brannigan-Leask, it uses the case of a fictitious home-care charity, Tullyvarne Care at Home, to ask what a good cause may do to be noticed, and what it risks when it lets software choose its staff.

Main idea — To fill its vacancies, the charity used a shock campaign around a major tournament it did not sponsor, then an automated system to sort the applications (l. 12-19, 39-43). Both choices brought attention and speed, but both put trust at risk: the trust of the public and of the event's sponsors, and the trust of applicants rejected without explanation (l. 20-38, 44-59).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A charity short of staff — Tullyvarne, a fictitious Belfast charity with about four hundred staff and as many volunteers, has some sixty empty posts, so visits are missed and older people wait for help (l. 1-4).
  2. A sector-wide shortage — In England, 6.2 per cent of adult social care posts were vacant in 2025/26, 8.7 per cent in home care; the typical care worker earned £12.60 an hour in March 2026, just above the legal minimum, and 44 per cent of home-care workers were on zero-hours contracts; overseas recruitment of care workers closed in July 2025, while the population aged 65 and over is expected to grow by 24 per cent by 2040 (l. 5-11).
  3. A shock campaign on borrowed ground — During a major (fictitious) tournament in Belfast, the charity ran billboards and an online video of an older woman waiting for help that never comes; without being an official partner, it parked advertising vans near the stadium and used the tournament's hashtag; the campaign aimed both to recruit and to make the public see older people's loneliness, a form of social marketing (l. 12-19).
  4. Success and backlash — The video was widely shared and thousands applied, but viewers complained to the ASA and the organiser defended its sponsors; the UK advertising code forbids fear or distress without good reason and shocking images used merely to attract attention, the ASA accepts some justified shock for important causes but asks for care with untargeted media, and the 2006 law for the London 2012 Games shows how organisers protect their sponsors (l. 20-29).
  5. The case for shock — For the director of communications, "Without shock, nobody looks" (l. 30): the tournament offered an audience no charity could afford, and the cause makes politeness "a luxury" (l. 31-32).
  6. The case against — For a lecturer in advertising, shock works only once, makes viewers feel guilty and turns older people into victims; ambush marketing may be legal and clever, but it is unfair to sponsors, and a charity's main asset is "its good name" (l. 33-38).
  7. Speed through software — To cope with the applications, the charity uses an applicant tracking system with screening questions and CV ranking; about two in three applicants were rejected automatically; the head of HR stresses the time saved and the same criteria for all (l. 39-43).
  8. The human cost — A woman who spent eight years caring for her mother was rejected by a one-line email, with no reason given; applicants with unusual careers argue that the job needs human qualities and that a machine may repeat old biases (l. 44-48).
  9. The regulator's warning — In November 2024, the ICO made almost 300 recommendations to firms that build AI recruitment tools: some allowed filtering by protected characteristics, guessed gender or ethnicity from names, or kept too much personal data indefinitely; candidates must be told how their data is used (l. 49-54).
  10. Trust, pay and hours — The chief executive has asked a recruiter to review every rejection and admits that no campaign will keep care workers without better pay and hours; what the charity cannot afford to lose is trust (l. 55-59).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Having summarised the article, I will now turn to the question: is it acceptable to shock people, or to borrow the spotlight of a major event, in order to get their attention, even for a good cause? I will first explain why shock and ambush marketing can be tempting and even useful for a charity, then show their costs for the public, the people shown and the organisation itself, before arguing that they are acceptable only within clear limits, and never as a substitute for real change."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le levier de problématisation est dans la question : « acceptable » peut s'entendre au sens légal (le code de la publicité, la protection des sponsors), moral (le respect des personnes représentées, la loyauté envers ceux qui paient) ou stratégique (l'attention gagnée devient-elle des recrutements durables ?). Le candidat distingue les deux procédés : la publicité choc porte sur le contenu, le marketing d'embuscade sur le lieu et le moment ; on peut accepter l'un et refuser l'autre. Un bon commentaire relie la question au second choix du texte : une campagne réussie qui produit des candidatures traitées sans égard abîme la confiance qu'elle voulait gagner (l. 39-48, 58-59). Aucune analyse juridique n'est attendue.

Plan A — Shock and ambush: tempting tools, high costs, strict conditions

  • Problematic: Can a good cause justify methods that would be criticised if a company used them?
  • I. Why charities shock and borrow spotlights: invisible causes and tiny budgets ("nobody looks", l. 30-31); social marketing to reveal a hidden reality, the loneliness of older people (l. 18-19); visible results (l. 20-21); the ASA itself accepts justified shock for important causes (l. 25-26); ambush marketing is often legal and can be creative (l. 36).
  • II. The costs: shock fatigue and guilt (l. 34-35); older people reduced to victims (l. 35); complaints and untargeted media that anyone may see, including children (l. 21, 26-27); unfairness to sponsors who pay (l. 22-23, 37-38); reputation, "a charity's main asset" (l. 37-38).
  • III. Conditions: a justified, proportionate, respectful message; careful placement; creativity rather than free-riding; follow-up with a fair and human recruitment process (l. 55-56); and better pay and hours (l. 56-57).
  • Conclusion: acceptable in limited doses and for a real purpose, but attention is only the first step; trust is what lasts (l. 58-59).

Plan B — Ends and means

  • I. The end: older people waiting for help, missed visits, a sector-wide shortage (l. 1-11); urgency can justify unusual means.
  • II. The means: shocking content (l. 13-15) and borrowed audience (l. 16-17); legal limits (l. 23-29) and ethical limits (l. 33-38).
  • III. The results: applications, but also complaints, conflict with the organiser, and rejected applicants who feel ignored (l. 20-23, 39-48); the means can undermine the end.

Plan C — Who pays for the attention?

  • I. The public: guilt, fatigue, distress, especially in untargeted media (l. 24-27, 34-35).
  • II. The people shown: older people as victims rather than citizens; carers and care workers made invisible (l. 13-15, 35-36).
  • III. Partners and applicants: sponsors and organisers (l. 22-23, 27-29); applicants attracted by the campaign, then rejected by software without explanation (l. 41-47); the charity's own good name (l. 37-38, 58-59).

Useful notions from the programme — 7.3.d shockvertising, ambush marketing, official sponsor, organiser, controversy, complaint, backlash, shock fatigue ; 7.3.b social marketing, awareness campaign, good cause, charity ; 7.3.a billboard, poster, advertising van, online video, hashtag, shares, untargeted media ; 5.3.a vacancy, job application, CV, applicant tracking system, screening questions, ranking, shortlist, recruiter, rejection ; 2.3.b personal data, protected characteristics, data retention, transparency, data protection regulator ; 2.3.c dehumanised recruitment, human review, automated decision (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les pourcentages et les dates du deuxième paragraphe sans dire qu'ils expliquent la pénurie.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the campaign was disgusting », « the software is racist »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : raconter la campagne ou le tri des candidatures sans dire si l'on peut choquer ou profiter d'un événement pour une bonne cause.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : réciter des définitions du marketing social ou du marketing d'embuscade sans lien avec le cas.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : analyser le droit de la publicité, le droit des marques ou la protection des données, détailler le fonctionnement d'un algorithme de tri, chiffrer une audience ou un coût par contact.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« six point two percents », « twelve pound sixty », « two thousand twenty-five slash twenty-six »).
  • Propre au sujet : croire que la campagne montre une personne âgée maltraitée (le texte décrit une femme qui attend, sans image dégradante, l. 13-15) ; croire que l'ASA a sanctionné la campagne (des plaintes lui ont été adressées, sans décision mentionnée, l. 21) ; croire que le marketing d'embuscade est toujours illégal (le texte dit qu'il est souvent légal, l. 36) ; attribuer les chiffres de Skills for Care à l'Irlande du Nord (ils portent sur l'Angleterre, l. 5) ; lire les 12,60 £ comme le salaire de Tullyvarne (c'est le salaire médian en Angleterre, l. 7) ; croire que l'ICO a contrôlé Tullyvarne (ses audits portent sur des concepteurs d'outils, en 2024, l. 49-51) ; confondre applicant tracking system et entretien vidéo ; dire the elderly ou old people avec mépris ; prendre Tullyvarne, le tournoi ou The Lagan & Foyle Business Beacon pour des entités réelles ; nommer des sponsors, des campagnes caritatives réelles ou des éditeurs de logiciels à la place de ceux du cas.
