Goodbye to "tick and bash"? A northern audit firm hands the basics to AI

Short of young accountants and watched by the regulator, a mid-tier firm is betting that AI will make audits more reliable and the job more attractive. Not everyone agrees.

By Philippa Rennard-Osei (fictitious author)

Every January, Chloe Varndell spent weeks in a client's back office, checking invoices against bank statements, one sample at a time. Auditors call it "tick and bash". Ms Varndell, a fictitious auditor in her twenties, qualified as a chartered accountant last year at Ashcombe Thewlis LLP, a fictitious mid-tier firm with about 5 four hundred partners and staff in Sheffield, Newcastle and Hull. Within months, she left. "It wasn't the ticking that drove me out," she says. "It was the busy season, and an empty office on Fridays. With everyone working from home, there was nobody to learn from." She now works in internal audit for a manufacturer, reviewing its risks and controls for the board. 10 External auditors, by contrast, work for the shareholders. They must be independent of the company and give an opinion on whether its accounts are fair. Her old firm wants to keep the next Chloe. It audits family businesses, charities, small companies and a handful of firms listed on a growth market. Like many rivals, it struggles to recruit trainees and to keep newly qualified 15 auditors. Its answer is technology. Since the spring, data analytics tools have tested every entry in a client's books rather than a sample, and an AI assistant writes first drafts of working papers for a human to check. "Sampling was the best we could do with paper," says Martin Alderthwaite, the firm's fictitious managing partner. "If software can look at the whole population, 20 the audit is more reliable. And nobody joins this profession dreaming of ticking boxes. Our juniors should be asking clients hard questions, not copying numbers." In July 2026, the regulator, the Financial Reporting Council (FRC), published its first combined Annual Review of Audit Quality. Of the 116 audits of listed companies, banks and other public interest entities it inspected in 2025/26, 79 per cent 25 needed no more than limited improvements. The six largest firms, which were responsible for most of the audits inspected, did far better than the others, where the figure was only 46 per cent, up from 38 per cent the year before. The FRC notes that some firms now use generative AI to perform audit work and write documentation, and it insists on human oversight. 30 In the year to March 2026, its enforcement team imposed £18.2 million of financial sanctions before discounts, up from £14.5 million, and opened seven investigations into auditors and accountants. A lack of professional scepticism was a recurring theme in the audit cases it closed. This worries Dr Aileen Draycott, a fictitious accounting researcher at the fictitious 35 Northfell University. "Firms are automating the training ground," she warns. "Juniors learnt what a strange invoice looks like by seeing thousands of normal ones. If the machine flags every anomaly, who will have the experience to judge them in ten years' time?" A tool nobody fully understands creates new risks, she adds: auditors may trust answers they cannot explain. "Software can test data. It cannot be sceptical, 40 and it cannot stand up to a client who pays the fee." Regulators, she points out, judge the quality of audits, not the size of the IT budget. Money matters too. The Big Four still dominate the market for large listed clients: according to the FRC, they audited 81.2 per cent of FTSE 250 companies in 2025. A mid-tier firm cannot match 45 their investment, so Ashcombe Thewlis rents its software from outside providers. The shortage may also be specific to audit. The FRC's latest figures show that accountancy students in the UK and Ireland rose by 0.9 per cent in 2025, to over 156,000, while the number known to be on the audit route fell by 4.3 per cent, to about 26,400. 50 America faces a similar problem. Accounting graduates fell by 6.6 per cent to 55,152 in 2023-24. After a rush of 42,626 new CPA candidates in 2023, ahead of a new exam, only 28,082 entered in 2024. Yet employment of accountants and auditors, about 1.6 million jobs in 2025, is projected to grow by 5 per cent between 2025 55 and 2035. The work is not disappearing; the young recruits are. Mr Alderthwaite says his trainees still test a few samples by hand in their first year, "so that they know what the machine is doing". Whether AI produces better auditors or simply fewer of them will only be clear when today's trainees become partners.

Question

To what extent will artificial intelligence make auditors more reliable rather than redundant?
