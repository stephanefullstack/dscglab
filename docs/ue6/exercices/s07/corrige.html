Note pédagogique : le texte oppose deux idées de la fiabilité. Pour l'associé gérant, elle vient de l'outil : tester toute la population des écritures plutôt qu'un échantillon (l. 18-21). 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'auteure, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres du FRC se regroupent en deux idées (la qualité reste inégale : les six plus grands cabinets font bien mieux que les autres, qui progressent toutefois ; les sanctions augmentent et visent souvent le manque d'esprit critique) au lieu d'être récités.

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Goodbye to 'tick and bash'? A northern audit firm hands the basics to AI", was published in The Tyne & Humber Business Gazette, a (fictitious) British business publication, on 14 November 2026. Written by Philippa Rennard-Osei, it uses the case of a fictitious mid-tier audit firm in the north of England, Ashcombe Thewlis LLP, to ask whether artificial intelligence can make audits more reliable and the profession more attractive to young accountants, or whether it removes the work through which they learn.

Main idea — The author presents the firm's bet that data analytics and AI will make audits more reliable and the job more attractive (l. 15-21), but she gives equal weight to the objection that machines remove the tasks that train auditors' judgement and cannot replace professional scepticism or independence (l. 34-41). She leaves the question open (l. 58-59).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A revealing departure — A young chartered accountant used to test invoices "one sample at a time" ("tick and bash", l. 1-3) and left her mid-tier firm within months of qualifying (l. 3-5). She blames the busy season and hybrid work, which left "nobody to learn from", rather than the repetitive tasks (l. 6-8).
  2. Internal and external audit — She now works in internal audit, reviewing a manufacturer's risks and controls for its board, whereas external auditors work for the shareholders, must be independent and give an opinion on the accounts (l. 8-11).
  3. The firm's problem and its answer — Ashcombe Thewlis struggles to recruit trainees and keep newly qualified auditors (l. 12-15); it now uses data analytics to test every entry rather than a sample, and an AI assistant to draft working papers that a human checks (l. 15-17).
  4. The managing partner's case — Testing the whole population makes the audit more reliable, and removing box-ticking makes the job more attractive: juniors should ask clients "hard questions" (l. 18-21).
  5. A demanding regulator — The FRC's first combined review of audit quality (July 2026) found that 79 per cent of the 116 audits inspected in 2025/26 needed no more than limited improvements; the six largest firms do far better than the others, where only 46 per cent reached that level (38 per cent the year before); some firms use generative AI, but the FRC insists on human oversight (l. 22-29). Financial sanctions rose to £18.2 million before discounts, against £14.5 million, and a lack of professional scepticism was a recurring theme (l. 30-33).
  6. The critical voice — A researcher fears that firms are "automating the training ground": juniors learnt to spot anomalies by seeing thousands of normal documents; a tool nobody fully understands creates new risks; software "cannot be sceptical" nor stand up to a client who pays the fee; regulators judge the quality of audits, not IT budgets (l. 34-41).
  7. An unequal market — The Big Four audited 81.2 per cent of FTSE 250 companies in 2025; a mid-tier firm cannot match their investment and rents its software (l. 42-45).
  8. A shortage specific to audit — UK and Irish accountancy students rose slightly (0.9 per cent, over 156,000), but the known number on the audit route fell by 4.3 per cent (l. 46-49). In the US, accounting graduates fell by 6.6 per cent, new CPA candidates dropped after a rush in 2023, yet employment is still projected to grow by 5 per cent: "The work is not disappearing; the young recruits are" (l. 50-55).
  9. An open conclusion — Trainees still test a few samples by hand to understand the machine; whether AI produces "better auditors or simply fewer of them" will only be clear in the long run (l. 56-59).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Having summarised the article, I will now turn to the question: to what extent will artificial intelligence make auditors more reliable rather than redundant? I will first show how AI can strengthen audit quality, then explain why reliability still depends on human judgement and independence, before discussing whether AI threatens auditors' jobs or rather changes how they are trained."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'auteure (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). « More reliable » invite à distinguer la fiabilité des tests et celle du jugement ; « rather than redundant » invite à distinguer la disparition des tâches et celle des emplois ; « auditors » désigne des personnes, pas seulement des cabinets.

Plan A — More reliable, as long as humans stay in charge

  • Problematic: Does AI make audits more reliable by itself, or only in the hands of well-trained, independent auditors?
  • I. AI can make audits more reliable: testing whole populations instead of samples (l. 15-20); faster detection of unusual entries; the FRC reports that AI-enabled techniques are increasingly common and that some firms now use generative AI (l. 28-29).
  • II. But reliability rests on human judgement: the FRC's recurring finding of a lack of scepticism (l. 32-33); a "black box" output that an auditor cannot explain (l. 38-39); independence from the client who pays the fee (l. 40-41); regulators hold people accountable, not software.
  • III. Not redundant, but different: entry-level tasks change, so training must be redesigned (hand testing in the first year, l. 56-58); in the US, demand for accountants and auditors is still projected to grow (l. 53-55).
  • Conclusion: AI makes auditors more reliable only if firms keep training juniors to question what the machine produces.

Plan B — By stakeholder

  • I. Young accountants: more interesting work earlier (l. 20-21), but fewer chances to learn by repetition (l. 35-37); image and working conditions (busy season, hybrid work, l. 6-8) matter as much as tasks; the audit route attracts fewer students (l. 48-49).
  • II. Audit firms: a way to do more with fewer people and to compete; but mid-tier firms cannot match the Big Four's investment (l. 42-45) and depend on outside software providers; the risk of a two-speed market.
  • III. Regulators, investors and the public: audit quality as a public good (l. 22-33); accountability for AI-assisted work; trust in the accounts of listed companies, charities and family businesses.

Plan C — Short term and long term, the UK and the US

  • I. In the short term, AI fills a gap: firms short of trainees can still audit every entry; the FRC's results are stable overall and improving at the smaller PIE audit firms, although they remain far behind the largest ones (l. 22-28).
  • II. In the long term, the risk is a missing generation of experienced auditors (l. 37-38, 58-59); the US shows that the pipeline can shrink while demand grows (l. 50-55).
  • III. The answer may be less technological than social: training programmes, apprenticeships, better busy-season conditions, a clearer image of audit as a career in the public interest; the candidate can compare with what they know of the French profession, without unsourced figures.

Useful notions from the programme — 4.4.a automation, data analytics, generative AI, sampling vs whole population, human oversight, "black box" ; 4.4.b talent shortage, pipeline, trainee, chartered accountant, CPA, retention, busy season, hybrid work ; 4.3.b audit quality, inspection, regulator, sanction, enforcement ; 4.3.a external vs internal audit, independence, professional scepticism, audit opinion ; 4.3.c the Big Four, mid-tier firm, market concentration (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les chiffres du FRC et de l'AICPA sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« AI will destroy jobs », « the researcher is right »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : parler seulement de la pénurie de comptables, ou seulement de la technologie, sans discuter la fiabilité des auditeurs.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : réciter des notions d'audit (assertions, seuils, procédures) sans lien avec la question.
