Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Two bosses, nobody listening? A pet food maker rethinks its grip on its plants", was published in The Upper Mississippi Commerce Examiner, a (fictitious) Minnesota business publication, on November 22, 2026. Written by Solveig Brandtmeier-Oakes, it tells the story of Halverline Pet Nutrition, a fictitious listed multinational from Minneapolis that makes dog and cat food, after falsified safety records at its Polish plant led to a recall, and asks how much control headquarters should keep over its subsidiaries.

Main idea — After a quality manager revealed that safety records had been changed to meet production targets, and after the company found that some supplier audits were a mere formality, its board is split: the chief executive wants to recentralize control, the whistleblower blames the culture and the targets, and an independent director defends local autonomy (l. 8-20, 36-54). The author concludes that clear accountability, realistic targets, independent checks and well-trained local managers matter more than the shape of the organization chart (l. 60-63).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A multinational in a matrix — Halverline sells in more than forty countries; its headquarters are in Minneapolis, but most of its nine thousand employees work in Brazil, Poland and Thailand; its supply chain spans three continents; each plant director reports to a global brand director and to a regional head (l. 1-7).
  2. The misconduct and the recall — The Polish plant's quality manager found that safety test records had been changed to keep lines running and meet targets; she warned the regional office twice, resigned and sent her evidence to the board; a recall followed in fourteen countries, including the United States: no confirmed illness, but food destroyed, shelves emptied, a falling share price and lasting reputational damage (l. 8-15).
  3. A second failure in the supply chain — Social audits of some suppliers of fish meal and poultry were a formality (announced visits, interviews in the manager's office, checklists signed in an hour); some suppliers' sustainability claims were never checked: due diligence stopped at the paperwork (l. 16-20).
  4. Scandals shape the rules — After Enron (2001), the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of July 2002 created the PCAOB to oversee the auditors of listed companies, required confidential channels for employees' concerns about accounting and protected employees who report fraud; since a 2010 law, the SEC has rewarded whistleblowers with 10 to 30 percent of sanctions above $1 million: about 27,000 tips and more than $60 million to 48 people in fiscal 2025 (l. 21-29).
  5. Food safety and whistleblower protection — The Food Safety Modernization Act of January 2011 moved the FDA from reacting to preventing, for animal as well as human food, and gave it the power to order a recall; a 2019 EU directive requires member states, including Poland, to protect people who report breaches of food and feed safety rules (l. 30-35).
  6. The chief executive's diagnosis — "Two bosses", so nobody owned quality, and both asked for volume; her plan: quality managers reporting directly to headquarters, unannounced audits of suppliers by outside firms, ethics and speak-up training for three hundred local managers; the hotline had been designed for accounting fraud only (l. 36-42).
  7. The whistleblower's answer — People did speak up, but the answer was "hit the numbers, fix it later"; if bonuses depend on volume, people cut corners, and training is useless if listening costs a manager a bonus: the problem is the culture and the targets (l. 43-48).
  8. The independent director's warning — Running everything through Minneapolis would slow the plants down; group rules cannot foresee everything; local teams know their markets, suppliers and regulators; treating them as suspects would drive the best away; decentralization did not cause the cover-up (l. 49-54).
  9. Nuances — Recentralizing would reassure investors and regulators for a while but cost speed and local knowledge; audits can remain formal whoever pays for them; ethics training can become a box to tick; whistleblower protection depends on the country, and enforcement priorities change with politics (l. 55-59).
  10. Conclusion — A multinational can neither control everything nor delegate everything: clear accountability, realistic targets, independent checks and local managers trained, and rewarded, for bringing bad news up matter more than the chart (l. 60-63).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Having summarized the article, I will now turn to the question: should a multinational trust its local managers, or control them from headquarters? I will first explain why headquarters cannot simply trust, then why control from the center has its own limits, before arguing that trust and control can work together."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06) ; les arguments du texte ne sont pas répétés sans analyse (JUR-C05). Le candidat gagne à distinguer deux sens de « control » (diriger, décider à la place de, ou vérifier, contrôler au sens de l'audit) et à noter que le siège n'est pas un arbitre neutre : les deux chefs du groupe réclamaient du volume et les primes dépendaient des tonnes expédiées (l. 38, 46). Il évite toute généralisation sur la Pologne, le Brésil ou la Thaïlande.

Plan A — Trust, but verify

  • Problematic: Is trust in local managers compatible with control from headquarters?
  • I. Why headquarters cannot simply trust. Distance and blurred accountability in a matrix (l. 6-7, 37-38); records falsified to meet targets (l. 9-10); supplier audits as a formality (l. 16-20); consumers, animals and shareholders pay the price (l. 12-15); regulators expect prevention (l. 30-32).
  • II. Why control from the center has limits. Slow decisions far from the market (l. 50-51); group rules cannot foresee everything (l. 51-52); local teams know customers, suppliers and regulators (l. 52-53); a culture of suspicion drives talent away (l. 53-54); both of the group's bosses asked for volume (l. 38).
  • III. Trust and control together. Independent checks (unannounced audits, direct quality reporting) that protect honest managers; realistic targets; training plus rewards for raising problems (l. 46-47, 62-63); whistleblower channels that actually reach the board.
  • Conclusion: a multinational should trust local managers to run the business, and control the few things that can harm consumers and the company: safety, ethics and the truth of the figures.

Plan B — The structure, the people or the incentives?

  • I. The structure: matrix, recentralization, decentralization. Two bosses dilute accountability (l. 37-38); a direct quality line to headquarters clarifies it (l. 38-39); but every structure has blind spots (the hotline built for accounting only, l. 41-42).
  • II. The people: managers and whistleblowers. Training in ethics and in speaking up (l. 40-41); the risk of box-ticking (l. 57-58); whistleblower protection and rewards (l. 25-29, 33-35), unequal from one country to another (l. 58).
  • III. The incentives: targets and bonuses. "Hit the numbers, fix it later" (l. 45); bonuses tied to tons shipped (l. 46); balanced objectives that include safety; leaders who reward bad news.

Plan C — What scandals teach multinationals

  • I. Scandals produce rules: Enron and Sarbanes-Oxley, the PCAOB, the SEC whistleblower program, food safety prevention, EU whistleblower protection (l. 21-35).
  • II. Rules are not enough: a hotline that ignores the labs (l. 41-42), audits that check paperwork (l. 16-20), enforcement that varies with politics (l. 59).
  • III. Culture as the last line of defense: local managers who are trusted to raise problems, and a headquarters that listens.

Useful notions from the programme — 1.2.a matrix structure, chain of command, dual reporting, accountability, centralization / decentralization, recentralize, span of control, stakeholders ; 1.2.b multinational, headquarters, subsidiary, home country / host country, global vs local, value chain, supply chain ; 1.4.b misconduct, falsified records, whistleblower, recall, cover-up, reputational damage, scandal ; 4.3.d Enron, Sarbanes-Oxley Act, PCAOB, audit committee, internal controls, whistleblower program ; 4.3.e social audit, ethical audit, unannounced audit, supplier audit, sustainability claim ; 5.1.c management training, ethics training, speak-up culture, coaching (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les dates d'Enron, de la loi Sarbanes-Oxley, de la loi de 2011 et de la directive de 2019 sans dire à quoi elles servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the chief executive is right », « matrix structures never work »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : décrire les structures d'entreprise ou les lanceurs d'alerte en général sans dire s'il faut faire confiance aux managers locaux ou les contrôler depuis le siège.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : réciter les types de structures ou le contenu de la loi Sarbanes-Oxley sans lien avec le cas.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : citer des articles de loi, détailler des procédures, des poursuites ou des sanctions pénales, expliquer des contrôles vétérinaires ou d'hygiène, parler de prix de transfert.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« twenty-seven thousands », « sixty millions dollars », « a three hundreds managers »).
  • Propre au sujet : attribuer la faute à la Pologne ou à une culture nationale (le texte dit que les salariés ont parlé, l. 43, et rattache la faute aux objectifs et à la matrice) ; croire que la directrice générale veut tout centraliser (elle recentralise la qualité et l'audit des fournisseurs, pas toutes les décisions, l. 38-41) ; croire que l'autrice de l'alerte a d'abord prévenu les autorités (elle a alerté deux fois le bureau régional, puis le conseil, l. 10-11) ; croire que la loi Sarbanes-Oxley vise la sécurité des aliments (elle porte sur l'information financière, l'audit et la fraude des sociétés cotées, d'où une ligne d'alerte « construite pour la fraude comptable », l. 41-42) ; croire que le programme de la SEC récompense tout signalement (seulement les informations qui mènent à plus de 1 million de dollars de sanctions, l. 26-27) ; confondre le PCAOB, qui contrôle les auditeurs, avec la SEC, qui réglemente les marchés ; confondre control (diriger) et check ou audit (vérifier) ; évoquer la composition du conseil ou les rémunérations des administrateurs comme sujet central (hors de l'angle du texte) ; nommer une entreprise, une marque ou un fournisseur réels, ou accuser une entreprise réelle pour une affaire récente ; prendre Halverline Pet Nutrition ou The Upper Mississippi Commerce Examiner pour des entités réelles.
