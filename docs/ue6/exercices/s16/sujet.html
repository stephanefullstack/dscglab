Two bosses, nobody listening? A pet food maker rethinks its grip on its plants

Falsified safety records at a Polish plant led to a recall in fourteen countries. Now a Minnesota pet food maker must decide how much freedom its subsidiaries deserve.

By Solveig Brandtmeier-Oakes (fictitious author)

Halverline Pet Nutrition, a fictitious company, sells dog and cat food in more than forty countries. Its headquarters are in Minneapolis and its shares trade in New York, but most of its nine thousand employees work far from Minnesota, in plants in Brazil, Poland and Thailand. Its supply chain, from fishing ports to 5 poultry farms, spans three continents. Like many multinationals, Halverline uses a matrix structure: each plant director reports both to a global brand director and to a regional head. Last spring, the quality manager of the Polish plant, Ewelina Kasprzycka-Lund, discovered misconduct: safety test records had been changed so that lines could 10 keep running and meet monthly targets. She warned the regional office twice, then resigned and sent her evidence to the board. In September, Halverline recalled several product lines in fourteen countries, including the United States. No illness had been confirmed by mid-November, but hundreds of tons of food were destroyed, retailers cleared their shelves and the share price fell 15 sharply. The reputational damage may outlast the recall. The internal investigation found a second failure. The social audits of some suppliers of fish meal and poultry were a formality: visits announced weeks ahead, workers interviewed in the manager's office, checklists signed in an hour. Some also claimed their fish came from audited, sustainable sources. Due 20 diligence at headquarters had stopped at the paperwork. Scandals have often shaped the rules. After the collapse of Enron in 2001, Congress passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in July 2002. It created the PCAOB to oversee the auditors of listed companies, asked audit committees to set up confidential channels for employees' concerns about accounting, and protected 25 employees who report fraud. Since a 2010 law, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has also rewarded whistleblowers whose tips lead to sanctions of more than $1 million, with 10 to 30 percent of the money collected. In fiscal 2025, it received about 27,000 tips and awarded more than $60 million to 48 people. 30 The Food Safety Modernization Act, signed in January 2011, shifted the focus of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) from reacting to problems to preventing them, for animal as well as human food, and gave it the power to order a recall. In Europe, a 2019 directive requires member states, including Poland, to protect people who report breaches of EU food and feed safety rules from dismissal or 35 demotion. Corinne Haldane-Voss, Halverline's chief executive, blames the organization chart. "The matrix gave every plant director two bosses, and nobody owned quality," she says. "Both bosses asked for volume." Her plan: plant quality managers will report directly to headquarters, outside firms will audit 40 suppliers without warning, and three hundred local managers will be trained in ethics and in how to raise a problem. "Our compliance hotline was built for accounting fraud," she admits. "Nobody thought of the labs." Ms. Kasprzycka-Lund is not convinced. "People in our plant did speak up. The warnings went up the chain of command for months, and the answer was always the 45 same: hit the numbers, fix it later," she says. "You can redraw the boxes, but if bonuses depend on tons shipped, people will cut corners. And no training helps if listening costs a manager a bonus. The problem is the culture and the targets." Teodoro Vasconcelos-Brink, an independent director, fears a different mistake. 50 "If every decision has to go through Minneapolis, many time zones away, the plants will slow down," he warns. "Group rules written in the home country cannot foresee everything. Our teams in each host country know their customers, their suppliers and their regulators. Treat them as suspects, and the best ones will leave." Decentralization, he argues, is not the cause of the cover-up. 55 Each side has a point. Recentralizing would reassure investors and regulators, for a while, but it would cost speed and local knowledge. Supplier audits can stay formal whoever pays for them, and ethics training can become one more box to tick. Protection for whistleblowers still depends on the country, and enforcement priorities change with politics. 60 A multinational can neither control everything from headquarters nor delegate everything to its subsidiaries. The shape of the chart matters less than clear accountability, realistic targets, independent checks, and local managers who are trained to bring bad news up, and rewarded for it.

Question

Should a multinational trust its local managers, or control them from headquarters?
