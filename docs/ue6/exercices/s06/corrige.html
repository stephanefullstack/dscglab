Note pédagogique : le texte oppose deux façons de donner la parole aux salariés : une voix collective et contractuelle (le syndicat, la convention collective, l. 14-18) et une voix managériale et participative (horaires choisis, primes, cadres de proximité formés, conseils de service, l. 20-26). La question élargit le débat au-delà de l'hôpital : le bon candidat ne se contente pas de choisir un camp, il distingue la voix que l'employeur accorde (et peut retirer, l. 56-57) de celle que les salariés détiennent, et il tient compte de la spécificité du soin (grève et sécurité des patients, l. 54-55). Le texte fait aussi dialoguer motivation intrinsèque (l. 23-24) et extrinsèque (l. 21, 25), ce que le candidat peut exploiter sans réciter de théorie : Maslow ou Herzberg peuvent être cités à l'appui d'un argument, ils ne sont pas exigés (fiche T5, § 7 ; JUR-C04). Aucune connaissance juridique précise n'est attendue (JUR-T05) : ni la procédure d'élection syndicale, ni le rôle contentieux du NLRB, ni les articles des lois du Michigan ; aucune comparaison chiffrée de salaires ; la politique de santé américaine reste un simple contexte.

2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'auteur, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les statistiques du BLS se regroupent en une idée (le syndicalisme recule en nombre d'adhérents mais redevient visible par les grèves) au lieu d'être récitées.

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Contract or trust? A Michigan hospital network meets its nurses' new union", was published in The Midwest Enterprise Monitor, a (fictitious) American business publication, on November 12, 2026. Written by Corinne Ashby-Mendez, it uses the case of a fictitious nonprofit hospital network in western Michigan, Kestrelmoor Health, whose nurses have just voted for a union, to ask whether employees need a union to be heard or whether better management could give them a voice.

Main idea — The author suggests that a union gives nurses a collective voice and written guarantees that even well-meaning managers cannot promise (l. 14-18, 56-57). However, she presents the managers' case for trust, meaning and participative management (l. 19-26), and the risks of a conflictual relationship, especially in a hospital (l. 51-55).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A personal turning point — An intensive care nurse, asked once again to work a second shift, refuses for the first time and starts organizing (l. 1-5); her colleagues have since voted for a union, a first for a network of about three thousand employees, and management must negotiate a first collective bargaining agreement (l. 6-9).
  2. The cause: a staffing crisis — Years of understaffing, mandatory overtime and departures; "Turnover fed burnout, and burnout fed turnover" (l. 10-13): nurses wanted enough staff to care for patients safely.
  3. The union's case: a guaranteed collective voice — A written contract (staffing levels, limits on overtime, a safe way to raise concerns) and a counterweight to the employer's power; "A good manager can listen to you … A contract means they have to" (l. 14-18).
  4. Management's case: trust, meaning and participation — The chief nursing officer accepts the diagnosis but not the remedy: the alternative plan (self-scheduling, retention bonuses, trained frontline managers, unit councils) relies on the meaning of the job and on trust; rigid rules may damage both; bonuses matter less than the leadership style of a unit manager (l. 19-26).
  5. A question of legitimacy — Who speaks for nurses: a former bedside nurse who now "answers to the board", or representatives who "answer to us"? (l. 27-30)
  6. A contrasting national context — Union membership has halved since 1983 (10.0 percent in 2025, 5.9 percent in the private sector, where nonprofit hospitals belong), yet major strikes are back (30 in 2025, far above the average), and most of the workers involved were in education and health services (l. 31-37); Michigan repealed its right-to-work law in 2023, a type of law that lets covered workers refuse to join or pay (l. 38-41).
  7. Nurses' leverage on the labor market — Many workers still quit (3.4 million in September 2026), median tenure is 4.1 years, and every experienced nurse who leaves takes know-how away (l. 42-45).
  8. The employer's responsibility — The wellness program treated the symptoms of burnout, not the cause; exhausted nurses make mistakes, so caring for staff is part of caring for patients (l. 46-50).
  9. Risks on both sides — A researcher warns that a hostile relationship can freeze the organization and that a hospital strike raises the question of patient safety; but participative management depends on the goodwill of those at the top, whereas a union does not (l. 51-57).
  10. An open conclusion — Management promises to bargain in good faith and keep the unit councils; the nurse no longer has to "hope they listen"; the outcome, "a truce or a battlefield", will shape how the network treats and keeps its staff (l. 58-61).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Now that I have summarized the article, I will turn to the question: are labor unions still the best way for employees to make their voice heard at work? I will first explain why unions remain a powerful and independent voice, then show that other channels, from participative management to direct communication, can also give employees a say, before arguing that the most effective voice combines collective rights with good everyday management."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'auteur (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). « Still » invite à discuter le déclin du syndicalisme ; « the best way » invite à comparer le syndicat avec d'autres canaux d'expression.

Plan A — An independent voice, but not the only one

  • Problematic: Can a voice granted by management ever replace a voice that employees own?
  • I. Unions remain a uniquely independent voice: a collective counterweight to the employer's power (l. 17-18); written, enforceable guarantees rather than goodwill (l. 16-17, 56-57); renewed visibility through strikes (30 major stoppages in 2025, l. 35-37).
  • II. But other channels can work, sometimes better: participative management, self-scheduling, unit councils and trained frontline managers (l. 20-26); the leadership style of the direct manager often decides whether people stay; in a hospital, a strike raises a patient-safety problem (l. 54-55) and an adversarial relationship can freeze change (l. 53-54).
  • III. The best voice combines both: collective rights as a safety net, good management as daily practice; management keeping the unit councils after the vote (l. 58) shows that the two are not exclusive.

Plan B — By stakeholder

  • I. Employees: voice, safety and dignity; intrinsic motivation (the meaning of care, colleagues) versus extrinsic rewards (bonuses, pay); the cost of union dues and the free-rider debate behind right-to-work laws (l. 39-41).
  • II. Employers: retention and the cost of turnover (l. 42-45); a union as an obstacle to flexibility or as a partner that makes problems visible; the role and training of frontline managers.
  • III. Society and patients: quality and safety of care (l. 49-50); the right to strike versus continuity of care; the duty of care of employers (wellbeing, CSR, l. 46-48).

Plan C — Intercultural: the US, the UK and France

  • I. The US: low and falling union membership (10.0 percent in 2025, 5.9 percent in the private sector), a revival of large strikes, and a state-by-state debate on right-to-work laws (Michigan's repeal in 2023).
  • II. The UK: trade unions with a long history in public services; the Employment Rights Act 2025, which received Royal Assent in December 2025 and includes provisions on trade unions and industrial action.
  • III. France: the candidate can draw on what they know of workplace representation and of bargaining beyond the company, and compare union membership with union influence, without unsourced figures.

Useful notions from the programme — 5.3.e labor union, collective bargaining, collective bargaining agreement (contract), union density, strike / work stoppage, right-to-work laws ; 5.1.d intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, sense of purpose, incentives, retention bonus ; 5.2.b retention, turnover, quits, tenure ; 5.1.a leadership style, participative management, frontline managers, authority, legitimacy ; 5.3.d wellbeing, burnout, duty of care, corporate social responsibility (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les statistiques du BLS sans dire à quoi elles servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the managers are right », « unions are outdated »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : parler seulement de l'hôpital ou de la pénurie d'infirmières sans discuter les autres moyens d'expression des salariés.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : réciter la pyramide de Maslow ou les facteurs d'Herzberg sans lien avec les infirmières du texte.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : procédure d'élection syndicale, rôle contentieux du NLRB, articles des lois du Michigan, préavis de grève dans la santé.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« ten percents », « three millions », « one thousand nine hundred eighty-three » pour 1983).
  • Propre au sujet : appliquer aux hôpitaux sans but lucratif le taux du secteur public (32,9 %) alors qu'ils relèvent du secteur privé (5,9 %, l. 33-34) ; croire que right-to-work signifie « droit au travail » (il s'agit de la liberté de ne pas adhérer ni cotiser) ; affirmer que l'abrogation du Michigan vaut pour tous les États ; traduire « syndicat » par syndicate ; prendre Kestrelmoor Health, ses soignants, Brantwood State University ou The Midwest Enterprise Monitor pour des entités réelles.
