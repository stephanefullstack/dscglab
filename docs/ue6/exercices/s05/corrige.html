Note pédagogique : le texte montre que l'influence est déjà traitée comme de la publicité sur deux points : le signalement (l. 25-36) et, pour les grandes entreprises, les restrictions sur les aliments moins sains (l. 20-21). Le bon candidat distingue donc deux niveaux de règles : dire que c'est une publicité (obligation générale) et limiter ce qui peut être montré, à qui et quand (restrictions sur le contenu et la diffusion). La question porte surtout sur l'écart entre la règle et son application (l. 38-41) et sur l'exemption des petites entreprises (l. 21-24, 47-49). L'exemption ne vaut que pour les restrictions de 2026 : les règles plus anciennes des codes britanniques, qui interdisent la publicité pour les produits trop gras, sucrés ou salés dans les médias destinés aux enfants ou particulièrement populaires auprès d'eux, s'appliquent à toutes les entreprises, PME comprises (guide de l'ASA sur les aliments moins sains, § 1.1.3 et 5.1.4) ; c'est aussi pourquoi la marque vise les 16-24 ans. Aucune connaissance juridique précise n'est attendue (JUR-T05) : ni analyse du DSA ou du RGPD (thème 2), ni profil nutritionnel, ni contentieux ; aucun calcul de rentabilité de la campagne ni évaluation de la marque (UE 4).

2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'auteur, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les règles du Royaume-Uni, des États-Unis et de l'UE se regroupent en une idée (le signalement est exigé partout, mais mal respecté) plutôt que d'être récitées l'une après l'autre.

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "No TV slot, fifty phones: a Leeds snack brand bets on influencers", was published in The Pennine Business Review, a (fictitious) British business magazine, on 9 November 2026. Written by Tamsin Whitlowe, it uses the case of a fictitious young snack brand from Leeds, Tarnwick Snacks, to examine whether influencer marketing is a smart way for a small brand to launch a product, and what risks it raises for young consumers and for the brand itself.

Main idea — The author suggests that influencer marketing allows a small brand to launch a product without traditional advertising, in a way that is cheaper and feels more authentic (l. 11-16). However, it raises questions about young people's ability to recognise advertising, about a legal exemption that small firms enjoy, and about the fragility of a brand's image (l. 45-55).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A launch through influencers — A student films herself tasting a new bar, with an "Ad" label from the first second; she is one of fifty paid micro-influencers (l. 1-4).
  2. The brand, the product and its positioning — A four-year-old Leeds brand with about twenty staff, investors and contract manufacturing launches an oat bar with less sugar for 16-to-24-year-olds; the positioning is "a treat with less sugar, not a health food" (l. 5-10).
  3. A deliberate choice against traditional advertising — No TV advert, billboard or paid banner (l. 11-12); for the co-founder, a TV campaign is too expensive and the target audience "hardly watch[es] live television" (l. 12-14); influencer marketing is "word of mouth on a large scale", with loyal audiences and authentic-looking videos (l. 14-16).
  4. A restricted channel — Since 5 January 2026 (previously set for 1 October 2025), adverts for less healthy food and drink are banned on UK television between 5.30am and 9pm and in paid-for online advertising at any time; paid influencer posts count as paid online advertising (l. 17-21); but food firms with fewer than 250 employees are exempt (l. 21-22), so Tarnwick would be exempt anyway (l. 23-24).
  5. Disclosure rules apply to everyone — In the UK, the ASA and CAP require an upfront "ad" label as soon as there is money, a free product or another incentive; "gifted", "collab" or "sponsored" are not clear enough; brand, influencer and agency are all responsible; 79 per cent of people want labels visible straight away (l. 25-32). The US FTC takes a similar line and warns that labels may not work with younger people (l. 33-36); the EU bans targeted adverts to children on platforms (l. 37-38).
  6. A gap between rules and practice — In an EU check of 576 influencers published in 2024, 97 per cent posted commercial content, but only one in five systematically disclosed it (l. 38-41); Tarnwick, for its part, imposes the label and checks every post (l. 42-44).
  7. Objections: young people and the exemption — A public health researcher argues that young people are bad at recognising advertising, "label or no label" (l. 45-47), and that the exemption lets a small firm do what the law forbids the giants: "Children's diets do not depend on the size of the company" (l. 47-49).
  8. A business risk: the brand image — For a young firm with no factory, brand image is the main asset; fifty creators mean fifty chances of a mistake, and "years of brand equity can be lost in a weekend" (l. 50-55).
  9. An open conclusion — The critic wants influencer content treated exactly like traditional advertising; the co-founder accepts it, because trust is what the brand cannot afford to lose; the outcome, "a national brand or a backlash", remains open (l. 56-59).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Now that I have summarised the article, I will turn to the question: should influencer marketing be regulated as strictly as traditional advertising? I will first show why influencer content is advertising and deserves the same protection for consumers, then explain why it cannot simply be regulated in the same way, before suggesting what fair and effective rules could look like."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'auteur (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). « As strictly as » invite à distinguer la sévérité des règles (mêmes principes) et les moyens de les appliquer (mêmes outils ?).

Plan A — The same principles, but adapted tools

  • Problematic: Is influencer content simply advertising in another form, or a new kind of communication that needs its own rules?
  • I. Influencer content is advertising: a brand pays and often controls the message (Tarnwick checks every post, l. 43-44); it looks like a friend's recommendation, so it is harder to recognise than a TV advert, especially for young people (l. 45-47); consumer protection should therefore be at least as strict (disclosure, no misleading claims, protection of minors).
  • II. But it is not traditional advertising: thousands of individual creators instead of a few broadcasters; small brands and small creators cannot afford heavy compliance; authenticity and word of mouth are part of its value (l. 14-16); the same rules (e.g. the 9pm watershed) do not translate easily to feeds that never stop.
  • III. Toward fair and effective rules: one clear label everywhere ("#ad" upfront, platform tools); shared responsibility of brands, creators, agencies and platforms; real enforcement (ASA lists of non-compliant influencers, CMA fines since April 2025, EU sweeps); a debate on whether health-based restrictions should depend on company size.

Plan B — By actor

  • I. Brands: influencer marketing as a launch tool for challengers (cost, reach, targeting of 16-to-24-year-olds, positioning); the brand as an intangible asset that can be built quickly and lost quickly (l. 50-55).
  • II. Influencers and platforms: creators as small businesses with a duty of transparency; platforms as gatekeepers of labels and of targeting rules (DSA ban on targeted ads to children).
  • III. Consumers and regulators: young people's media literacy; public health and the fight against unhealthy diets; regulators' limited means, which favour self-regulation and co-regulation (ASA and CAP in the UK).

Plan C — Intercultural: the UK, the US, the EU and France

  • I. The UK: self-regulation backed by law (CAP Code enforced by the ASA, consumer law enforced by the CMA), plus statutory restrictions on less healthy food advertising since January 2026 with an SME exemption.
  • II. The US: the FTC's Endorsement Guides and liability of both brand and influencer; a ban on fake reviews (2024); stronger protection of children's data for targeted advertising (COPPA, 2025).
  • III. The EU and France: the DSA's ban on targeted ads to children; EU sweeps showing poor compliance (2024); a dedicated French law on commercial influence (June 2023): France chose a specific statute, the UK and the US mostly rely on general advertising rules.

Useful notions from the programme — 7.3.b influencer marketing, sponsored content, disclosure, endorsement, micro-influencer ; 7.2.b product launch, new product, challenger brand ; 7.2.c target audience, segmentation, positioning ; 7.1.e brand image, brand equity, brand trust, intangible asset ; 7.3.a traditional vs online advertising, TV advert, paid-for online advertising, watershed (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les règles britanniques, américaines et européennes l'une après l'autre sans dire à quoi elles servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« influencers are dishonest »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : décrire le marketing d'influence en général sans le comparer à la publicité classique ni parler de régulation.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours sur le marketing mix ou sur le RGPD (thème 2), sans lien avec le cas.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : profil nutritionnel, articles du DSA, règles du CAP Code citées par numéro.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« five hundred seventy-six » est toléré, « one on five » pour one in five ne l'est pas ; « 9 p.m. » lu « nine hours »).
  • Propre au sujet : affirmer que le produit de Tarnwick est interdit de publicité (le texte ne le tranche pas) ; confondre signalement de la publicité (pour tous) et restrictions sur les aliments moins sains (pour les entreprises de 250 salariés ou plus) ; croire que l'exemption permet à une PME de viser les enfants (les règles plus anciennes des codes s'appliquent à toutes les entreprises) ; dire que le DSA interdit toute publicité vue par des mineurs (il interdit la publicité ciblée destinée aux enfants) ; prendre Tarnwick Snacks, ses dirigeants ou The Pennine Business Review pour des entités réelles.
