2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'auteur, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03).

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Trust over tick-boxes: can Britain's boardrooms keep policing themselves?", was published in The Ledger Weekly, a (fictitious) British business weekly, on 12 October 2026. Written by Fenella Brightwater, it deals with British corporate governance and asks whether the UK's "comply or explain" approach, based on codes and shareholder pressure rather than binding law, can still work now that sustainability is on the board's agenda.

Main idea — The author argues that the British self-regulatory model has achieved real results where progress is easy to measure and publish (board diversity, pay votes), but that it may reach its limits with sustainability, which could require "a firm legal deadline" (l. 58).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. Starting point: how the model works — Listed companies follow the UK Corporate Governance Code or explain publicly why they do not; shareholders, not inspectors, enforce it (l. 1-6). The 2024 Code is a set of principles, applying since 2025, with a new statement on internal controls expected for financial years starting from January 2026 (l. 7-10).
  2. First argument: diversity as the success story — Women held 9.5 per cent of FTSE 350 board seats in 2011; with voluntary targets and no fines, 69 per cent of these companies exceeded 40 per cent women by the end of 2025, and 98 FTSE 100 companies had at least one ethnic minority director in March 2026 (l. 11-18). The EU, by contrast, chose a directive: a 40 per cent target for non-executive seats by June 2026, backed by binding, transparent selection rules (l. 19-22).
  3. Second argument: executive pay — A binding vote on pay policy at least every three years since October 2013 and an annual advisory vote make boards accountable through embarrassment rather than sanctions; a fictitious trustee sums up the investor view: information and a vote are enough (l. 23-30).
  4. The hardest test: sustainability reporting — The UK published voluntary sustainability reporting standards in February 2026 with no compulsory date, while the EU keeps a mandatory directive, although narrowed by the Omnibus (more than 1,000 employees and €450 million in turnover), and the US securities regulator stopped defending its climate rules in March 2025 (l. 31-39).
  5. Counter-argument — Critics say that the model works only for what is easy to count: diversity targets coexist with few women CEOs, pay policies are approved despite a widening gap with ordinary wages, and voluntary climate reports can be "long, glossy and empty" (l. 40-46).
  6. Cultural explanation — Britain relies on codes, investors and public reviews; continental Europe on written law and quotas; the US on regulation that changes with each administration (l. 47-52).
  7. Conclusion of the author — Voluntary codes work "when someone is watching" (l. 53-54); sustainability needs comparable data and probably a legal deadline, the very thing the model was designed to avoid (l. 55-58).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Having summarised the author's views, I will now turn to the question: to what extent can voluntary codes, rather than binding laws, make British companies more accountable on diversity, pay and sustainability? I will first show what soft law has achieved, then examine its limits, before suggesting that the real answer may lie in a combination of both."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'auteur (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le candidat n'a pas à connaître les textes juridiques : une date et un exemple suffisent (JUR-T05).

Plan A — Yes, but… (achievements vs limits)

  • Problematic: Is "comply or explain" a genuine accountability tool or a convenient way to avoid binding rules?
  • I. Voluntary codes can be powerful: flexibility, ownership by business, speed (diversity figures, l. 13-18); investors as enforcers (say on pay, stewardship: the UK Stewardship Code 2026 applies to asset managers and owners from January 2026); reputational pressure through annual public reviews.
  • II. But they reach their limits where outcomes are hard to measure or costly: few women CEOs, pay gaps, greenwashing risk in voluntary climate reports (53 % of green claims vague or misleading according to the European Commission's study); no sanction when the explanation is weak.
  • III. Hence a hybrid answer: codes for culture and behaviour, law for disclosure and deadlines (the UK already mixes both: binding say on pay inside company law, identity checks for directors at Companies House since November 2025).
  • Conclusion: voluntary codes make companies accountable only when backed by transparency and, in the last resort, by law.

Plan B — By issue (diversity / pay / sustainability)

  • I. Diversity: the clearest success of voluntary targets, compared with the EU directive (40 % of non-executive seats or 33 % of all seats by 30 June 2026) and national quotas such as France's 2011 law; yet the pipeline to executive roles remains thin.
  • II. Pay: a mixed record; the binding vote is in fact a law, which shows that the UK model is not purely voluntary; shareholder revolts at AGMs are visible but rarely decisive.
  • III. Sustainability: the weakest area; voluntary UK standards versus the EU's mandatory (if narrowed) CSRD and the US retreat; investors need comparable data, which voluntary reporting rarely provides.

Plan C — By culture and system (UK / EU / US)

  • I. The British tradition: principles-based governance and investor stewardship; strengths and blind spots.
  • II. The continental model: written law and quotas; a level playing field, but the Omnibus shows that binding rules can also be rolled back under competitiveness pressure.
  • III. The American swing: rules that change with administrations (SEC climate rules abandoned in March 2025; foreign bribery enforcement paused in February 2025); markets and states fill the gap. Opening: is convergence possible through global standards (ISSB)?

Useful notions from the programme — 1.1.b board composition, independence, diversity, say on pay ; 1.3.b CSRD and Omnibus in principle and dated, ISSB, UK standards ; 1.4.c regulations and their limits (uneven enforcement, cost, backlash) ; shareholder vs stakeholder view (1.1.c) in one sentence only (pas de placage de cours, JUR-C04).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui juxtapose diversité, rémunération et durabilité sans montrer la logique de l'auteur (succès mesurables, puis limite).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : parler de la RSE en général sans traiter « voluntary codes versus binding laws ».
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : exposer la théorie de l'agence ou les seuils de la CSRD au lieu de répondre.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : citer des articles de loi ou des seuils non demandés.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« nine comma five », « the 12 October »).
  • Propre au sujet : confondre shareholder et stakeholder ; confondre pourcentage de sociétés (69 %) et pourcentage de sièges (40 %) ; présenter le Royaume-Uni comme soumis à la CSRD.
