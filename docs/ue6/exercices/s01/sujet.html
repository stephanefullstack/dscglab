Trust over tick-boxes: can Britain's boardrooms keep policing themselves?

Britain prefers codes and shareholder pressure to binding quotas. The approach has transformed board diversity, but sustainability may prove a harder test.

By Fenella Brightwater (fictitious author)

Every spring, the annual general meetings of Britain's largest companies put a very British idea to the test. Shareholders gather, directors explain themselves, and the rules of the boardroom are enforced less by inspectors than by investors. The principle even has a name: "comply or 5 explain". A listed company either follows the UK Corporate Governance Code or tells its owners, in public, why it has chosen a different route. The latest version of the Code, published in January 2024, presents itself as a set of principles rather than a rulebook. It has applied to financial years starting on or after 1 January 2025; from the 2026 financial year, 10 boards are also expected to say whether their key internal controls work. Supporters argue that this flexible model has delivered results that laws elsewhere struggled to achieve. Boardroom diversity is their favourite example. In 2011, women held just 9.5 per cent of board seats in FTSE 350 companies. Business-led reviews then set voluntary targets, with 15 no fines attached. By the end of 2025, 69 per cent of those companies had gone beyond the goal of 40 per cent women on their boards. A separate review on ethnic diversity reported in March 2026 that 98 companies in the FTSE 100 had at least one director from an ethnic minority background. The European Union chose a different path. Its directive on women on 20 boards set listed companies a target of 40 per cent of non-executive seats by June 2026, enforced through binding selection rules. Britain, which left the EU before the directive was adopted, has stuck to persuasion. Executive pay tells a slightly different story. Since October 2013, shareholders in listed companies have had a binding vote on the remuneration policy 25 at least every three years, alongside an annual advisory vote on what directors were actually paid. A large protest vote rarely sinks a pay package, but it embarrasses the board, and a chair who ignores it usually faces a tougher meeting the following year. "Investors do not need a regulator to tell a board it has gone too far," says Gareth Oyinlola-Price, 30 a fictitious pension fund trustee. "They need clear information and a vote." Sustainability reporting is where the British taste for persuasion faces its hardest test. In February 2026, the government published UK standards for sustainability reporting, built on a global baseline, but left them voluntary, with no date yet for making them compulsory. Across the Channel, 35 the EU's reporting directive is mandatory, although a simplification package agreed in December 2025 narrowed it to companies with more than 1,000 employees and over €450 million in turnover. In the United States, the securities regulator voted in March 2025 to stop defending its own climate disclosure rules in court, leaving investors and individual states to fill the gap. 40 Yet critics say the British model works best for what is easy to count. A board can meet a diversity target while women remain rare as chief executives. A remuneration policy can be approved even when pay rises far faster than wages on the shop floor. And a voluntary climate report can be long, glossy and empty. "Explaining is cheap," says Priya Castellane, a 45 fictitious campaigner for responsible investment. "Without a deadline, the explanation quietly becomes the strategy." The divide is partly cultural. Britain trusts a dense network of codes, institutional investors and public reviews to discipline its companies. Much of continental Europe prefers written law and quotas, while American 50 regulation tends to swing with each change of administration. Each system reflects a different answer to the same question: who should hold directors to account, and with what tools? The real lesson of the British experiment may be that voluntary codes work when someone is watching. Diversity improved because reviews published 55 names and numbers year after year, and investors read them. If climate and nature are to follow the same path, shareholders will need comparable data. That may require the one thing "comply or explain" was designed to avoid: a firm legal deadline.

Question

To what extent can voluntary codes, rather than binding laws, make British companies more accountable on diversity, pay and sustainability?
