Charter or check? A Carolina furniture maker's family weighs its future

A private equity fund wants to buy a family-owned furniture maker and grow it fast. Its president would rather write the company's duties to its town into its charter.

By Nadia Pemberton-Ruiz (fictitious author)

In the finishing room of Hollowbrook Furniture Company, Darnell Fairleigh-Moss runs his hand along a walnut dresser. His grandfather sanded tables in the same building. "Around here, everybody has a cousin at Hollowbrook," he says. The fictitious family-owned firm employs about twelve hundred people in two plants in 5 a small town in the North Carolina foothills. Such loyalty has become rare in American furniture making. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), factories making furniture and related products employed about 680,000 people in 2000, but only about 338,000 in 2025. Hollowbrook has survived decades of cheaper imports by selling solid-wood furniture made to order. 10 This fall, a private equity fund offered to buy a majority stake from the family. It promises to double the size of the business, partly by offshoring its cheaper ranges. For some of the twenty-odd family shareholders, most of whom have never worked in the plants, it is a chance to cash in. "Our grandparents built this company to provide for their family," says 15 Dalton Brannock-Ivey, a cousin who lives in Atlanta. "Shareholders are the owners. We have every right to get the best price for what we own." To him, shareholder primacy is common sense. A mission written into the charter, he adds, would only scare away the next buyer. Claire Brannock, the third-generation president who also chairs the board of 20 directors, wants the opposite. She proposes turning Hollowbrook into a public benefit corporation, or PBC. Delaware, where the company is incorporated, has allowed this status since 2013. A PBC is still a for-profit company, but its charter must name one or more public benefits it intends to produce. Its directors must balance the shareholders' financial interests, the interests of those 25 materially affected by its activity, and that public benefit. At least every two years, it must report to its shareholders on its objectives, how it measures progress and its results: in effect, an impact report. Hollowbrook's mission would have two parts: keeping skilled manufacturing jobs in the town and buying wood only from sustainably managed forests. "A promise lasts 30 until the next owner," Ms. Brannock says. "A charter is harder to tear up." She presents the plan as the triple bottom line in practice. For people, the company runs a paid apprenticeship with the local community college and trains workers at its own learning center. For the planet, it buys certified hardwood, burns its sawdust to heat the drying kilns and has put solar panels on 35 one plant's roof. For profit, it charges more than importers and accepts thinner margins. "We actually sold more dining tables once we explained where the wood came from," she says. Supporters see other benefits. A clear mission helps to recruit young people who might otherwise leave for the city, and it builds a customer loyalty that 40 advertising cannot buy. Mr. Fairleigh-Moss is blunter: "If they sell to a fund, I'll eventually be training my replacement overseas." The family quarrel echoes a national debate. In August 2019, 181 chief executives signed a Business Roundtable statement committing their companies to all their stakeholders, breaking with earlier versions that put shareholders first. Since 45 then, an ESG backlash has made stakeholder capitalism a political target. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) adopted climate disclosure rules on March 6, 2024, then put them on hold. On March 27, 2025, it voted to stop defending them in court. For Ms. Brannock, this is one more argument: whatever happens in Washington, the report of a PBC is required by state law. 50 Miriam Oduya-Strand, a fictitious professor of corporate governance at the equally fictitious Larkwood State University, is less convinced. "A legal status does not plant a single tree," she says. "It can become a shop window, another form of greenwashing." She also notes that only shareholders can take the board to court over its duty to balance interests, not the workers or the town it 55 is meant to protect. "A company that stops making a profit protects no jobs at all," she adds. "The best protection for workers may simply be patient owners, whatever the legal form." The family will meet in December to decide. If Ms. Brannock wins, Hollowbrook's promises to its town will be written into its charter. Yet, as her critics 60 note, only the people who hold the shares will be able to enforce them.

Question

Should a company's first duty be to its shareholders or to all of its stakeholders?
