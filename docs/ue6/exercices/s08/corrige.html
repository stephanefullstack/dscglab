2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres du BLS servent le contexte (une industrie qui a perdu la moitié de ses emplois), les dates de la SEC servent l'argument de la présidente (le rapport de la PBC ne dépend pas de Washington) : ils se disent avec leur fonction, sans être récités.

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Charter or check? A Carolina furniture maker's family weighs its future", was published in The Tar River Business Weekly, a (fictitious) American business publication, on November 13, 2026. Written by Nadia Pemberton-Ruiz, it uses the case of a fictitious family-owned furniture maker in North Carolina, Hollowbrook Furniture Company, to ask whether a company should be sold to the highest bidder or bound by a mission written into its charter.

Main idea — The article suggests, mainly through the president's voice (l. 28-30, 48-49), that writing a mission into a company's charter protects its workers, its town and the environment better than a promise, but it also shows the limits of a legal status: it guarantees no action, it can be used as a shop window, and only shareholders can enforce it (l. 50-60).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A company rooted in its town — Hollowbrook employs about twelve hundred people in two plants in a small town; families have worked there for generations (l. 1-5).
  2. A shrinking industry — Jobs in American furniture making fell from about 680,000 in 2000 to about 338,000 in 2025; Hollowbrook survived cheaper imports by selling solid-wood furniture made to order (l. 6-9).
  3. The offer that divides the family — A private equity fund wants a majority stake; it promises to double the business, partly by offshoring its cheaper ranges; for cousins who have never worked in the plants, it is a chance to cash in (l. 10-13).
  4. The case for selling: shareholder primacy — The owners have the right to the best price; a mission in the charter would scare away buyers (l. 14-18).
  5. The president's alternative: a public benefit corporation — A for-profit company whose charter names public benefits; directors must balance shareholders' financial interests, the interests of those affected and the mission; a report to shareholders at least every two years, "in effect, an impact report" (l. 19-27).
  6. The thesis: a charter rather than a promise — The mission would protect skilled local jobs and sustainably managed forests; "A promise lasts until the next owner … A charter is harder to tear up" (l. 28-30).
  7. The triple bottom line in practice — People (paid apprenticeship, training center), planet (certified hardwood, sawdust for the kilns, solar panels), profit (higher prices, thinner margins, sales helped by explaining where the wood comes from) (l. 31-37).
  8. The advantages of CSR — Recruiting young people, customer loyalty that advertising cannot buy; a worker fears offshoring (l. 38-41).
  9. A national debate — The Business Roundtable statement of 2019 broke with shareholder primacy; since then, an ESG backlash; the SEC adopted climate disclosure rules in March 2024 and stopped defending them in March 2025; a PBC's report, by contrast, is required by state law (l. 42-49).
  10. Counter-arguments — A professor of governance warns that a legal status does not plant a tree and can become greenwashing; only shareholders can go to court over the balancing duty; a company without profit protects no jobs; patient owners may matter more than the legal form (l. 50-57).
  11. An open, ironic conclusion — If the president wins, the promises will be in the charter, but only shareholders will be able to enforce them (l. 58-60).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Now that I have summarized the article, I will turn to the question: should a company's first duty be to its shareholders or to all of its stakeholders? I will first explain why shareholders have a strong claim to come first, then show that a company cannot last without its other stakeholders, before arguing that the real issue is how to make long-term promises credible."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). « First duty » invite à discuter un ordre de priorité ; un candidat peut défendre la primauté de l'actionnaire, à condition de traiter les objections.

Plan A — Shareholders first, stakeholders always

  • Problematic: Is there a real choice between serving shareholders and serving stakeholders, or only a question of time horizon?
  • I. Shareholders have a strong claim: they own the company and bear the risk; profit is the condition of survival ("A company that stops making a profit protects no jobs at all", l. 55-56); clear accountability (one objective is easier to measure than many); Friedman's view, in one sentence.
  • II. But a company depends on all its stakeholders: skilled workers, a town, forests, loyal customers (l. 28-41); Freeman's view and the Business Roundtable statement of 2019 (l. 42-44); the cost of ignoring them (offshoring, loss of know-how, reputation).
  • III. Long-term value reconciles both: patient owners (l. 56-57), family firms, legal forms that write a purpose into the charter (PBC, French société à mission, British CIC), or company law that asks directors to consider stakeholders (UK).

Plan B — How can stakeholders be protected? Law, ownership, transparency

  • I. Through the law: the PBC (l. 19-27) and its equivalents abroad; strength: a promise that survives a sale (l. 29-30); limit: only shareholders can enforce it (l. 53-55, 58-60).
  • II. Through ownership: family shareholders, cooperatives, employee ownership, patient capital vs a private equity fund looking for growth and an exit (l. 10-13).
  • III. Through transparency and markets: impact reports (l. 25-27; under Delaware law, a statement to shareholders, public only if the charter or bylaws say so), labels such as B Corp (notion, not a legal form), consumer choice (l. 36-37); limit: greenwashing (l. 52-53) and the retreat of federal climate disclosure in the US (l. 45-48).

Plan C — The three pillars (economic, social, environmental)

  • I. Profit: higher prices and thinner margins (l. 35-36); can a responsible company stay competitive against cheaper imports (l. 6-9)?
  • II. People: local jobs, training and apprenticeships (l. 31-33); offshoring and the fear of "training my replacement overseas" (l. 40-41); the town as a stakeholder.
  • III. Planet: sustainably managed forests, certified wood, energy (l. 33-35); limits: a status "does not plant a single tree" (l. 51-52).

Useful notions from the programme — 1.1.c shareholder primacy, stakeholder capitalism, purpose, Business Roundtable statement ; 1.3.a CSR, triple bottom line (people, planet, profit), mission ; 1.3.c apprenticeship, community investment, certified wood, renewable energy ; 1.4.a reputation, employer branding, customer loyalty, greenwashing, ESG backlash, cost of CSR ; 3.4.a impact report, sustainability disclosure, mandatory vs voluntary reporting ; 1.1.a family business, succession, private equity (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui juxtapose les positions des membres de la famille sans montrer l'articulation (une offre, deux visions, un contexte, des objections, une conclusion ironique).
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the cousins are greedy », « the president is right »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : raconter le cas de Hollowbrook ou définir la PBC sans discuter le devoir premier de l'entreprise.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : exposer longuement Friedman, Freeman ou la théorie de l'agence au lieu de les utiliser en une phrase au service d'un argument.
