Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

  1. la synthèse du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
  2. le commentaire organisé du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un entretien en anglais de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

Found by whom? Bristol's makers weigh life among the giants

A search update and cheap copies test how far a makers' cooperative should rely on Big Tech.

By Freya Llewin-Cotter (fictitious author)

Near Bristol's harbour, about sixty staff run Thrumwell, a fictitious online marketplace for handmade ceramics, textiles and woodwork. It belongs to its sellers: some three thousand makers, each with one vote, form a platform cooperative. This month its board asked: should Thrumwell stay on the big platforms, leave them, or 5 trust the regulator to tame them? E-commerce lets a Somerset weaver sell a blanket in Leeds or Lisbon. Online sales reached 29.2 per cent of retail sales in Great Britain in October 2026, according to the Office for National Statistics. Yet most customers find Thrumwell through a search engine, a social media advert or its app, downloaded from one of two app 10 stores. In the spring, a major search engine changed its algorithm. Within weeks, Thrumwell slid down the search rankings and its sales fell by about a fifth. Meanwhile, factory-made copies of members' work appeared on a global marketplace, far cheaper and shipped from the other side of the world. 15 Dominic Asante-Hale, the fictitious chief executive, is blunt: "A handful of firms decide who gets seen, and they write the rules alone." Big Tech thrives on network effects: the more buyers a platform attracts, the more sellers it draws, and vice versa, until it becomes a gatekeeper between small firms and their customers. He wants to spread the risk across newsletters, galleries and craft fairs, and to back 20 tougher regulation. Britain's digital markets regime came into force on 1 January 2025: the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) can give "strategic market status" to a firm with substantial and entrenched market power in a digital activity. In October 2025, it did so for the leading search service and the two main mobile platforms. In June 25 2026, it gave the search firm six months to rank results on objective and non-discriminatory criteria and to warn businesses of significant changes. "Good," says Mr Asante-Hale, "but by the time the rules bite, some of our makers will have closed." Tax also rankles. Since April 2020, search engines, social media platforms and 30 online marketplaces in groups earning over £500 million worldwide and £25 million in the UK from these services have paid a 2 per cent digital services tax on their UK revenues. A government review in November 2025 found that it raised around £800 million in 2024-25 and that some groups passed part of the cost on to their customers, who, for a marketplace, include its sellers. The tax is meant to be 35 temporary; the 15 per cent global minimum tax on multinationals with revenues above €750 million, now in force in the UK, does not replace it. Bethan Morrow-Iyer, a fictitious ceramicist from the Forest of Dean, found her best-selling bowl copied in July. In the UK, design right protects a shape automatically, as copyright protects original work; registering a design costs a fee 40 but gives up to 25 years of protection. "In practice, I would have to prove the copy and pay to fight it," she says. Most respondents to a government consultation, reported in November 2026, put the typical cost of a design dispute above £50,000. Ministers will explore a small claims route for simpler registered-design cases, but will not make copying an unregistered design a crime. "The platforms take a fee on 45 every copy. Why would they hurry?" Callum Ferriday, a fictitious former executive of a large global marketplace, disagrees. "Platforms gave a potter in Gloucestershire a shop window on the world for the price of a commission," he says. "Walk away from them and you walk away from the customers." Co-ops have small marketing budgets, he adds, and many lookalikes 50 are legal: a style cannot be owned, and a counterfeit fakes a brand. "Shoppers choose price and convenience." Going it alone means handling personal data. Targeted advertising on social media reaches people the platforms know to like crafts; a mailing list grows slowly. The Information Commissioner's Office reminds small businesses that marketing emails 55 need consent or a "soft opt-in" and an easy way to unsubscribe, and that targeting adverts with cookies requires consent. Members also make a social and green case: fair prices keep rare skills alive, and a bowl made and sent within a day's drive travels less than a copy shipped across the world. In January, they will vote on a plan to stay on the platforms but let none 60 bring in more than a third of sales. "We can't leave the giants," says Mr Asante-Hale, "but we can stop handing them the only key to our shop."

Question

Can small businesses thrive in a digital economy dominated by a handful of platforms?
