Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

  1. la synthèse du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
  2. le commentaire organisé du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un entretien en anglais de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

Old friends, new engine: a Welsh campervan maker reinvents its best-seller

A family firm near Swansea bets on an electric van and online subscriptions. Its dealers and oldest customers are wary.

By Elinor Prysedd (fictitious author)

Every August, hundreds of campervans with green pop-up roofs fill a field above the sea in South Wales. Most of their owners are retired. The rally is run by the owners' club of Gwennoral Coachcraft, a fictitious family firm based near Swansea, and has long been its best advertisement. 5 Gwennoral, run by the third generation, employs about three hundred people. It buys vans from large manufacturers and fits them with beds, a kitchen and wooden furniture from its own workshop. Its flagship model has been built on the same diesel van for some twenty years and is sold through a dealer network of about thirty independent firms across the UK. Gwennoral has hardly ever paid for an 10 advert: word of mouth and customer loyalty did the job. The formula is running out of road. The flagship's sales have fallen for several years, its buyers are getting older and the rules are tightening. Under a plan confirmed by the UK government in April 2025, new petrol and diesel vans may be sold until 2035; from then on, every new car and van must be zero emission. In product 15 life cycle terms, the best-seller has passed maturity and entered decline. Rhodri Gwennoral, the fictitious chief executive and the founder's grandson, will not wait. Next spring the firm launches an electric campervan designed in-house, along with weekly rental and a monthly subscription, sold online to younger customers who may never own a van. Selling use rather than ownership has a name: 20 servitisation. "You cannot save a product in decline with new cushions," he says. "We have to innovate, reach a new generation and sell the experience as much as the vehicle." The brand, he argues, has never sold metal and wood: its value proposition is freedom, the joy of the journey and skilled craftsmanship. The timing looks right. According to provisional Welsh Government figures, overnight 25 holiday trips to Wales by residents of Great Britain rose by 20 per cent in 2025, to about 3.4 million. Yet trips based on camping, caravans or glamping fell by 9 per cent in Wales and by 18 per cent across Great Britain. And since June 2025, drivers with an ordinary car licence may drive a zero-emission vehicle of up to 4.25 tonnes in Great Britain, above the usual 3.5-tonne limit, without extra training: room for 30 heavy batteries. For a firm of this size, the electric van is a disruptive innovation, not a new trim level. If it works, Gwennoral could gain a first-mover advantage among British converters; rivals may prefer to follow, spending less and learning from its mistakes. 35 Not everyone is convinced. Ceri Abernant-Wolde, a fictitious dealer in the West Midlands, has sold Gwennoral vans for twenty-five years and feels bypassed. "Dealers built this brand. We service the vans, take back the old ones and know the customers by name," she says. "Now the factory wants to sell over our heads." The firm long relied on a push strategy, with dealers stocking vans and selling them on; the new 40 plan pulls customers towards the brand itself, through its website and advertising. She also doubts the product: a loaded van has a limited range, and charging points remain scarce in rural areas, "exactly where our customers want to go". Nia Brackwell-Osei, the fictitious marketing director, joined last year from a large consumer brand. "Word of mouth only reaches people who already know us," she says, 45 "and the club is not renewing itself." She is rethinking the marketing mix: one product, three prices to buy, rent or subscribe, two routes to market, and the firm's first real advertising campaign. Some club members see it as a betrayal of the brand's heritage: Gwennoral "never needed to shout". Zero marketing, they argue, kept costs low and the brand authentic. She promises loyal owners the first test 50 drives, and part-exchange stays with the dealers. But she will not sell "a zero-emission holiday": "The van has no exhaust, but the batteries have a footprint." In the workshop, electric vans require new skills; the firm promises to train its carpenters and fitters, not replace them. Some owners add that a diesel van running 55 for thirty years is sustainable too. Others suggest a middle road: a new version of the old diesel model, or new markets abroad, could extend its maturity. Mr Gwennoral is not tempted. "We are not selling a different dream," he insists, "just a different way to reach it." The club will judge next August, in the field above the sea.

Question

Can a heritage brand reinvent itself without losing the customers who made it?
