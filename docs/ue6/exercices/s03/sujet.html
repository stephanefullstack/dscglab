Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

Fewer coats, no bonfires: a Midlands brand gets ahead of Europe's rules

Europe now bars big brands from destroying unsold clothes. A staff-owned British label is applying the rule early. Critics doubt it will curb overproduction.

By Imogen Thackwray (fictitious author)

On a grey morning in October, the rails in a warehouse near Dublin are full of last winter's coats. A few years ago, many of them would have ended up in an incinerator. Today they are checked, repaired and sent back out to outlet sales, charities or resale shops. "Nothing leaves this building to be burnt," says 5 Declan Mulvenna, the warehouse's fictitious manager. The warehouse belongs to Kestrel Lane Clothing, a fictitious maker of city and outdoor wear founded in the East Midlands. It has about two hundred staff, shops in England, Scotland and the Republic of Ireland, and an online store. The Dublin site serves its customers in the European Union. 10 What has changed is the law. Since 19 July 2026, large companies in the EU may no longer destroy unsold clothes, accessories and shoes. Under the bloc's ecodesign rules, which came into force in 2024, they must first try to sell the goods, even at a discount, donate them or prepare them for reuse, and report every year what they still discard. Medium-sized firms will follow from 2030. 15 The problem is not small. The European Environment Agency estimates that 4 to 9 per cent of all textiles put on the European market are destroyed before anyone wears them, or between 264,000 and 594,000 tonnes a year. Each person in the EU consumed 19 kg of textiles in 2022, up from 17 kg in 2019, and threw away 16 kg. Less than 15 per cent of that waste was collected separately. The agency puts the 20 climate cost at 355 kg of carbon dioxide equivalent per person each year. For fast fashion, such figures are the price of a business model. Brands launch new ranges every few weeks, order huge volumes to keep unit costs low and treat overstock as a normal write-off. Ultra-fast online sellers push the logic further. As a medium-sized firm, Kestrel Lane will not be covered until 2030, yet it already 25 applies the rule to all its stock, in Britain as well as in Ireland. Great Britain has no similar ban, although retail groups say the government is considering one. The choice owes much to the way the company is owned. Since its founder sold her shares, an employee ownership trust has held most of the capital on behalf of the staff. There are no outside investors asking for faster growth every quarter. "Our 30 owners are the people who sew, pack and sell," says Rosalind Penhallow, the firm's fictitious chief executive. "They would rather sell fewer coats at full price than chase volume and burn the rest." The change has reached the factory floor. Instead of mass production in long runs, the brand now makes smaller batches in its own East Midlands workshop and tops up 35 popular lines during the season. About a fifth of its outerwear is made to order: customers wait two weeks, and nothing is cut before it is sold. Deadstock fabric is turned into bags, and a take-back scheme collects worn garments in its shops. Europe has a second lever. The revised Waste Framework Directive, in force since 16 October 2025, makes extended producer responsibility compulsory for textiles and 40 footwear. Producers will pay a fee on each item they sell to fund collection, reuse and recycling, with lower fees for durable, recyclable goods. Member states have until June 2027 to turn it into national law. Not everyone is convinced. "A ban on burning clothes is not a ban on making too many," argues Anjali Wrexford, a fictitious campaigner for sustainable fashion. She 45 fears that brands will simply move the problem: deeper markdowns, more donations and more exports. The EU already exported 1.4 million tonnes of used textiles in 2023, and much of what cannot be reused abroad ends up in open landfill. Critics raise other doubts. Small batches cost more per garment, and many shoppers still choose the lowest price. In the UK, almost half of all used textiles, 50 49 per cent, went into general waste in 2021, while the country consumed 1.42 million tonnes of new textiles in 2022. And circularity remains marginal: in 2024, only 12.2 per cent of the materials used in the EU came from recycling. Kestrel Lane's managers admit that their model is no blueprint for the whole industry. But they believe the direction is set. If destroying stock is no longer 55 an option, the cheapest garment becomes the one that is never made.

Question

Can regulation succeed where the fashion industry has failed, and put an end to overproduction?
