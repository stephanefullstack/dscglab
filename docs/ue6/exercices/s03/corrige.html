Note pédagogique : la question porte sur la surproduction, pas seulement sur la destruction des invendus. Le bon candidat voit l'écart entre les deux : la loi interdit de brûler les stocks, elle ne plafonne pas les volumes. Aucune connaissance juridique précise n'est attendue (JUR-T05) : pas d'actes délégués, de dérogations ni de seuils chiffrés de l'« entreprise moyenne » ; les dates suffisent. Aucune formule de gestion des stocks n'est attendue (UE 3).

2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'auteur, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres de l'Agence européenne pour l'environnement servent à mesurer le problème : il faut en retenir deux ou trois, pas les réciter tous.

Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Fewer coats, no bonfires: a Midlands brand gets ahead of Europe's rules", was published in The Mercantile Observer, a (fictitious) British business newspaper, on 19 October 2026. Written by Imogen Thackwray, it uses the case of a fictitious clothing brand from the East Midlands, Kestrel Lane Clothing, to examine the new European rules against the destruction of unsold clothes and their limits.

Main idea — The author suggests that EU rules are starting to change the economics of overproduction in fashion: a staff-owned brand that is not yet covered already applies them and produces less, in smaller batches. However, critics doubt that a ban on destroying stock will reduce the volumes produced (l. 43-52, 53-55).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A concrete case — In a warehouse near Dublin, unsold coats are no longer burnt but repaired, resold or donated (l. 1-5); the company is a medium-sized British brand with shops in Britain and Ireland (l. 6-9).
  2. The cause: a new EU rule — Since 19 July 2026, large companies in the EU may no longer destroy unsold clothes, accessories and shoes; under the ecodesign rules (2024) they must sell, donate or prepare goods for reuse and report what they discard; medium-sized firms will follow from 2030 (l. 10-14).
  3. The scale of the problem — 4 to 9 per cent of textiles put on the European market are destroyed before use (264,000 to 594,000 tonnes a year); EU consumption rose to 19 kg per person in 2022; less than 15 per cent of textile waste is collected separately; 355 kg of CO2 equivalent per person (l. 15-20).
  4. The fast-fashion model — Overstock is built into a model of frequent new ranges and huge volumes; ultra-fast online sellers go further (l. 21-23).
  5. An early adopter and why — Although not covered until 2030 and with no equivalent ban in Great Britain, Kestrel Lane applies the rule to all its stock (l. 24-26); its employee ownership trust frees it from outside investors seeking growth (l. 27-32): "They would rather sell fewer coats at full price than chase volume" (l. 31-32).
  6. A new production model — Smaller batches, in-season top-ups, a fifth of outerwear made to order, deadstock reused, a take-back scheme (l. 33-37).
  7. A second EU lever — The revised Waste Framework Directive, in force since 16 October 2025, makes extended producer responsibility compulsory for textiles: fees on each item, lower for durable and recyclable goods, national law by June 2027 (l. 38-42).
  8. Objections — The ban may only move the problem (markdowns, donations, exports: 1.4 million tonnes of used textiles exported in 2023, partly ending in open landfill) (l. 43-47); small batches cost more and shoppers want low prices; in the UK 49 per cent of used textiles went into general waste in 2021; circularity is marginal (12.2 per cent in the EU in 2024) (l. 48-52).
  9. Conclusion of the author — The model is "no blueprint" (l. 53), but the direction is set: "the cheapest garment becomes the one that is never made" (l. 55).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Now that I have summarised the article, I will turn to the question: can regulation succeed where the fashion industry has failed, and put an end to overproduction? I will first show how the new rules change the incentives of fashion brands, then explain why a law alone cannot end overproduction, before suggesting what else is needed."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'auteur (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). La formule « put an end to » invite à nuancer : réduire n'est pas mettre fin.

Plan A — Regulation can change incentives, but not end overproduction alone

  • Problematic: Can laws make overproduction unprofitable, or only less visible?
  • I. Regulation changes the economics: destruction is no longer a cheap way out; reporting creates transparency; EPR fees make producers pay for the end of life and reward durable goods; trade rules target ultra-fast imports (a temporary €3 EU customs duty on low-value parcels since 1 July 2026).
  • II. Its limits: the problem moves (markdowns, donations, exports to Africa and Asia); medium-sized firms are covered only from 2030 and Great Britain has no ban; enforcement and fraud; nothing caps volumes; consumers keep buying cheap clothes.
  • III. What must come with it: new business models (small batches, made-to-order, resale, repair), governance that rewards the long term (employee ownership), consumer change and honest green claims (CMA action against fashion retailers, 2024).

Plan B — By actor

  • I. Lawmakers: the EU's "rules first" approach (textiles strategy of 2022, ban, EPR); its reach beyond Europe through the single market.
  • II. Companies: from mass production to smaller batches; cost and margin issues; why ownership matters (an employee ownership trust vs listed fast-fashion groups under pressure for volume growth).
  • III. Consumers and workers: low prices, frequent purchases, second-hand markets; garment workers in supplying countries (Rana Plaza, 2013) — a law on waste does not address working conditions.

Plan C — Intercultural: Europe, Britain and the global supply chain

  • I. The EU: binding bans and producer responsibility.
  • II. Britain after Brexit: no ban, a voluntary and consultative approach; British firms selling in the EU must still comply ("Brussels effect").
  • III. The rest of the world: production and used clothes cross borders; overproduction may simply shift to markets with weaker rules.

Useful notions from the programme — 6.2.f fast fashion, ultra-fast fashion, garment workers, textile waste, de minimis exemption ; 6.2.d excess inventory, markdowns, write-off, destruction of unsold goods ; 6.2.e waste, circular economy, eco-design ; 6.1.c mass production, batch production, mass customisation ; 1.1.a employee ownership as a governance mode (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les chiffres de l'Agence européenne pour l'environnement sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the ban is a great idea »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : décrire l'interdiction sans se demander si elle réduit la production.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours sur l'économie circulaire ou sur la gestion des stocks (formules, coûts de stockage : hors sujet en UE 6).
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : seuils de l'entreprise moyenne, dérogations, actes délégués, architecture du reporting de durabilité.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« two hundred sixty-four thousand » sans and en anglais britannique est toléré, mais « one comma four two » pour 1.42 ne l'est pas).
  • Propre au sujet : confondre interdiction de détruire et interdiction de produire ; dire que l'interdiction s'applique déjà à Kestrel Lane ; affirmer que le Royaume-Uni a adopté la même règle ; prendre Kestrel Lane Clothing ou The Mercantile Observer pour des entités réelles.
