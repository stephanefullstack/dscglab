Presenting the document — This article, entitled "Slides before filings? A reactor-parts stock after the frenzy", was published in The Allegheny Markets Sentinel, a (fictitious) Pennsylvania business publication, on November 20, 2026. Written by Corliss Ambergate-Rowe, it tells the story of Varnholt Reactor Works, a fictitious listed company near Pittsburgh that makes parts for small nuclear reactors, to ask who is responsible when a share price races ahead of a company that has not yet sold anything.

Main idea — Carried by retail investors and by the story of electricity for data centers, the shares of a company with no revenue rose about sevenfold, then lost two-thirds of their value when its annual report postponed its first delivery (l. 6-8, 19-25). The chief financial officer says the company followed the rules, a retail investor blames optimistic voluntary announcements and weak protection, and a private equity fund offers to take the company off the market (l. 38-52). The author concludes that the blame is shared (l. 60-63).

Logical structure of the argument

  1. A company with a future but no revenue — About four hundred workers in a former steel mill make vessels, heat exchangers and modules for small modular reactors; Varnholt went public some eighteen months ago, but its first delivery is still to come (l. 1-5).
  2. A bubble built on a story — From February to August, the share price rose about sevenfold, pushed by retail investors on brokerage apps and forums; the story (data centers need electricity, nuclear power is low-carbon) rests on official figures: about 4.4 percent of U.S. electricity in 2023, 6.7 to 12 percent possible by 2028 (l. 6-11).
  3. Public money for nuclear power — The Department of Energy offered $900 million in March 2025 and picked two teams for up to $800 million in December 2025; in June 2026, it made a conditional commitment of $17.5 billion in loans for parts of ten large reactors; supporters see green funding, critics point to waste, safety and the gap between a loan offer and an order (l. 12-18).
  4. Voluntary disclosure, then the annual report — Monthly investor presentations, guidance for a delivery "in the spring" and a climate pledge, none of them required; then the 10-K delays the first delivery by about a year, recalls the losses and announces a need for new money; the shares crash, losing two-thirds of their value in three weeks (l. 19-25).
  5. The rules of the game — The SEC requires an annual 10-K (business, risk factors, audited financial statements), quarterly 10-Qs and 8-Ks for certain major events, usually within four business days; since 2000, Regulation Fair Disclosure has required companies to make public what they tell analysts or some shareholders: a level playing field (l. 26-32).
  6. Climate, a separate case — The SEC's climate disclosure rules of March 2024 were put on hold, no longer defended from March 2025 and proposed for rescission on May 29, 2026, with no final decision by mid-November; Varnholt's pledge is voluntary and unverified (l. 33-37).
  7. The chief financial officer's defense — Everything was filed on time and by the rules; the risk factors had warned for a year; "the market read our slides, not our filings"; the listing paid for a new welding hall and kept the mill open (l. 38-42).
  8. The retail investor's anger — The delay was in "the small print"; management fed the excitement with announcements it did not have to make; the regulator protects big funds better than ordinary savers (l. 43-47).
  9. The private equity offer — A fund offers to take Varnholt private, above today's price but far below the peak; a company with no revenue and a ten-year cycle "does not belong on the stock market"; private capital is more patient (l. 48-52).
  10. Nuances — Bubbles are old and rarely have one cause; they can fund useful infrastructure; more rules would not stop enthusiasm and would make listing harder; a take-private may hurt or help small shareholders; private capital is less transparent; voluntary climate information is only useful if checked (l. 53-59).
  11. Conclusion — The blame is shared: companies should put required filings before the story, investors should read before they buy, and the regulator should make facts equal and easy to read (l. 60-63).

Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05) — "Having summarized the article, I will now turn to the question: is the company, its investors or the regulator to blame when a share price runs far ahead of the business? I will first show what each of them did wrong, then explain why a bubble is more than the sum of individual faults, before suggesting how responsibility could be better shared."

3. Commentaire : plans possibles

Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'auteur (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le candidat gagne à distinguer ce qui est obligatoire (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K) de ce qui est volontaire (présentations, prévisions, engagement climatique), et la légalité d'une communication de sa loyauté. Il évite tout chiffrage de la valeur de la société, de l'offre du fonds ou de la perte de l'investisseur : le texte n'en donne pas, et ce n'est pas l'objet de l'épreuve.

Plan A — Three actors, one shared responsibility

  • Problematic: Who should do what, so that a share price stays closer to the business?
  • I. The company: legal but not always fair. Everything was filed (l. 39-40), but voluntary presentations, guidance and a climate pledge (l. 19-21) fed the story; the bad news came in the risk factors and financial pages of a long document (l. 22-25, 44-45). The question of "tone": a company can be within the rules and still mislead by emphasis.
  • II. The investors: "read before you buy". Retail investors relied on apps and forums (l. 7-9); the 10-K was public and free (l. 26-28); buying near the peak with savings is a personal decision; but small investors lack time and analysts (l. 46-47).
  • III. The regulator: equal facts, but readable facts? Level playing field and Regulation Fair Disclosure (l. 30-32); yet length and complexity make filings hard to read; climate rules on hold, then proposed for rescission (l. 33-36); investor education (Investor.gov) as a complement.
  • Conclusion: the blame is shared, but not equally: the company controls its message, so it carries the first duty; the regulator sets the minimum; investors choose their risks.

Plan B — A bubble is more than a fault

  • I. Bubbles are old and collective: crowd psychology, fear of missing out, a credible story backed by real figures (l. 8-11), public money (l. 12-16); "rarely have a single cause" (l. 53).
  • II. A bubble can be useful: capital raised at high prices builds factories (l. 41-42); railroads and fiber-optic networks (l. 53-54); a strong IPO market (cf. exemples datés) — but losses fall on those who bought last (l. 43-44).
  • III. Limiting the damage: clearer disclosure, guidance that stays close to the facts, better investor education; but more rules would not stop enthusiasm and would make listing harder for young firms (l. 54-56).

Plan C — Public or private: where should a company with no revenue raise money?

  • I. The case for listing: access to many investors, visibility, financing of the mill and jobs (l. 41-42); transparency imposed by the 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K (l. 26-30).
  • II. The case for private capital: patience over a ten-year cycle (l. 50-52), no quarterly pressure, fewer forums; but less transparency (l. 57-58) and a take-private that may hurt or help retail shareholders (l. 56-57).
  • III. Public support as a third way: grants and loans for low-carbon power (l. 12-16), green funding debated (l. 16-18); public money as a signal that can also feed a bubble.

Useful notions from the programme — 3.2.d bubble, crash, speculation, volatility, peak, frenzy, fear of missing out, dot-com bubble ; 3.2.b annual report, 10-K, risk factors, losses, guidance, profit warning ; 3.2.c disclosure, compulsory / voluntary, quarterly reporting (10-Q), current report (8-K), investor presentation, transparency ; 3.2.e regulator, Securities and Exchange Commission, Regulation Fair Disclosure, level playing field, investor protection ; 3.3.a IPO, going public, listing, private equity, take-private, delisting, patient capital ; 3.4.b green funding, loan, conditional commitment, low-carbon energy, transition finance (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

  • JUR-T02 — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
  • JUR-E02 — synthèse linéaire qui récite les chiffres des centres de données et des aides publiques sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
  • JUR-E04 — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the investor is naive », « nuclear power is the future »).
  • JUR-C03 — éluder la question : décrire la Bourse ou le nucléaire en général sans dire qui est responsable quand un cours devance l'entreprise.
  • JUR-C04 — placage de cours : réciter les étapes d'une bulle ou le contenu d'un 10-K sans lien avec le cas.
  • JUR-T05 — technicité excessive : valoriser la société, chiffrer la perte de l'investisseur ou la prime du fonds, détailler les procédures de la SEC ou le fonctionnement d'un réacteur.
  • JUR-E08 — lecture de notes rédigées.
  • JUR-L06 — chiffres et dates mal dits (« seventeen point five billions », « nine hundred millions dollars », « a thirty-four-years-old »).
  • Propre au sujet : croire que la société a caché le retard (il figurait dans le 10-K et ses facteurs de risque l'annonçaient comme possible, l. 22-24, 40) ; croire que tout événement important impose un 8-K : le formulaire ne vise que certains événements énumérés, et un retard de livraison n'en fait pas nécessairement partie, d'où la défense de la directrice financière (l. 39-41) ; le candidat n'a pas à connaître les rubriques du formulaire ; confondre les 17,5 milliards de prêts (engagement conditionnel pour dix grands réacteurs, l. 14-16) avec une commande à Varnholt ; lire 6,7 à 12 % comme la part actuelle des centres de données (c'est une projection pour 2028, l. 11) ; affirmer que la règle climatique de la SEC est en vigueur, ou déjà abrogée (elle est suspendue et proposée à l'abrogation, sans décision définitive à la mi-novembre, l. 33-36) ; confondre le capital-investissement (private equity), qui achète des entreprises pour les transformer, avec les placements spéculatifs, sortis du programme ; donner un conseil d'achat ou de vente ; nommer une société, un fonds ou une application de courtage réels ; prendre Varnholt Reactor Works, Corlenmoor Capital Partners ou The Allegheny Markets Sentinel pour des entités réelles.
