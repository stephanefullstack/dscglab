Slides before filings? A reactor-parts stock after the frenzy

A Pennsylvania reactor-parts maker soared on the data-center boom, then crashed on its own annual report. Now a private equity fund wants to buy it.

By Corliss Ambergate-Rowe (fictitious author)

In a former steel mill south of Pittsburgh, about four hundred workers make steel vessels, heat exchangers and modules for small modular reactors. Varnholt Reactor Works, the fictitious company that owns the mill, went public about eighteen months ago. It has no real revenue yet: its first delivery, to 5 an unnamed reactor developer, is still to come. This year, retail investors fell for Varnholt: from February to August, its share price rose about sevenfold. On brokerage apps and online forums, buyers repeated one story: data centers need huge amounts of electricity, and nuclear power can supply it with very little carbon. A Department of Energy report of 10 December 2024 estimated that data centers used about 4.4 percent of U.S. electricity in 2023 and could use 6.7 to 12 percent by 2028. Washington helps, too. In March 2025, the department offered $900 million to help deploy small modular reactors; in December 2025, it picked two teams for up to $800 million, with new power expected in the early 2030s. In June 2026, 15 its lending office made a conditional commitment of $17.5 billion in loans to buy long-lead parts for ten large reactors. Supporters call this green funding for low-carbon power; critics point to waste and safety, and note that a loan offer is not an order. Varnholt's managers rode the wave, with investor presentations almost every 20 month, guidance for a first delivery "in the spring" and a pledge to run the mill on carbon-free electricity within five years. None of this was required. Then, in late September, came the annual report on Form 10-K. In its risk factors and financial pages, Varnholt said the first delivery would slip by about a year, recalled its losses and warned that it would need new money next 25 year. The shares crashed, losing two-thirds of their value in three weeks. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) sets the rules. Every year, listed companies file a 10-K, which describes the business, lists the main risk factors and includes audited financial statements, plus quarterly reports on Form 10-Q. Certain events must be disclosed on Form 8-K, usually within four 30 business days. Since 2000, Regulation Fair Disclosure has required a company that shares important news with analysts or some shareholders to make it public. The goal is a level playing field: the same facts for every investor. Climate is another matter. The SEC adopted climate disclosure rules on March 6, 2024, then put them on hold during court challenges. In March 2025, it 35 voted to stop defending them; on May 29, 2026, it proposed to rescind them. No final decision had been announced by mid-November. Varnholt's climate pledge remains voluntary, and unverified. Renata Szczepanik-Hale, Varnholt's chief financial officer, denies that the company fed a bubble. "We filed everything on time and by the rules," she 40 says. "Our risk factors have warned for a year that the schedule could slip. The market read our slides, not our filings." The listing, she adds, paid for a new welding hall and kept the mill open. Trevor Koenigsmark, a thirty-four-year-old electrician from Johnstown, bought near the peak. "Every presentation said 'spring.' The delay was in the small 45 print of a document nobody reads on a phone," he says. "They kept the excitement going with announcements they did not have to make. The regulator protects big funds better than people like me." Last week, Corlenmoor Capital Partners, a fictitious private equity fund, offered to take Varnholt private at a price above today's but far below the 50 peak. "A company with no revenue and a ten-year cycle does not belong on the stock market, where every quarter is an exam," says Anjali Brennecke-Moss, a partner at the fund. "Private capital is more patient." Bubbles are as old as markets, and rarely have a single cause. They can also fund useful infrastructure, as railroads and fiber-optic networks did. More 55 rules would not stop enthusiasm, and would make listing harder for young companies. A take-private, and the delisting that follows, may hurt retail investors who sell at the bottom, or give them a way out. Private capital is also less transparent. And voluntary climate information has value, but only if someone checks it. 60 When a share price runs ahead of the business, the blame is shared. Companies should put their required filings before their story; investors should read before they buy; and the regulator should make sure that the facts are equal for all, and easy to read.

Question

Is the company, its investors or the regulator to blame when a share price runs far ahead of the business?
