Clean opinion, messy numbers: a Kentucky vertical farm rewrites its past (thème 4, anglais américain)

Épreuve orale – Durée : 30 minutes hors préparation (exposé : 15 minutes maximum ; entretien : 15 minutes maximum) – Coefficient : 1

Durée conseillée : 1 h de préparation, puis 15 min d'exposé et 15 min d'entretien

Document autorisé : aucun. Préparation d'une heure sans autre document que celui fourni avec le sujet (pas de dictionnaire).

Thème du programme : 4 – Accounting and auditing

Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

  1. la synthèse du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
  2. le commentaire organisé du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un entretien en anglais de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

Le document est un article de presse fictif rédigé pour l'entraînement à partir de faits vérifiés ; la publication, le titre, l'autrice, la société, le cabinet d'audit, les chaînes de supermarchés et les personnes citées sont fictifs.

Clean opinion, messy numbers: a Kentucky vertical farm rewrites its past

Its new internal auditors found sales booked too early and costs parked on the balance sheet.

By Corinna Albescu-Hart (fictitious author)

Quillmere Greens, a fictitious company, grows lettuce, herbs and microgreens under LED lamps at four vertical farms around Louisville, Kentucky. Listed on the Nasdaq, it employs about nine hundred people and sells to regional supermarket chains. It has never made a profit, but it promised investors it 5 would turn a profit next year. Last month, it said it would restate its accounts for the past two fiscal years. Its shares lost almost half their value in a day. Indoor farming is still a niche: U.S. growers sold about $1 billion of food crops grown under cover in 2024, says an Agriculture Department census published in February 2026. 10 The errors were found not by the outside auditor but by a new internal audit team, set up last spring at the audit committee's request. "We report to the audit committee, not to the people whose numbers we check," says Marisol Quintero-Blake, who heads the team. The first problem is revenue recognition. Some deliveries were invoiced in the 15 last days of a quarter and booked as sales, but a supermarket chain later sent part of them back. Discounts owed to grocers were recorded months late. Revenue landed in the wrong period. The second problem is costs that were capitalized too generously. The cost of getting a new farm running, and part of the work on its growing software, were recorded as assets, not expenses. The useful lives of 20 growing equipment were stretched, which cut the yearly depreciation charge. Each choice made losses look smaller. A restatement is a warning. In 2022, staff at the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) reminded companies that management is responsible for the financial statements: when a serious error is found in published accounts, 25 investors must be told promptly. Chief executive Tobias Wennerholm-Price defends his record. "A young company building something new has to make estimates: how long a farm will last, when a customer has really bought," he says. "Ours were too hopeful, but honest, and we corrected them ourselves. The technology works." 30 Priya Venkataram-Cole, a fund manager in Cincinnati, is not convinced. "Accounts that change after the fact are useless for making decisions," she says. "A company that is not yet profitable should favor prudence over optimism." Its lenders want explanations, and one supermarket chain has suspended a contract. The external auditor, a mid-sized audit firm, had given a clean opinion on both 35 years. In October 2026, the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB), which inspects the auditors of listed companies, reported that at the Big Four, the share of inspected audits where the auditor had not gathered enough evidence fell from 26 percent in 2023 to 8 percent in 2025. At smaller U.S. firms outside the big networks, it was still 32 percent. In March 2025, PCAOB staff had warned 40 that estimates are among the riskiest areas of an audit, open to management bias. "We are here every day," Ms. Quintero-Blake says. "We saw trucks come back. An external auditor visits a few weeks a year and tests samples. It finds such things only if it asks hard questions." 45 Gideon Asante-Rowe, a former auditor who teaches accounting in Lexington, spreads the blame. "Management must give a fair picture. The audit committee must challenge it. The auditor must keep its professional skepticism, even with a big client." For a mid-sized firm, he notes, one large client's fees can matter a lot, so independence must be protected. "And an audit gives reasonable 50 assurance, not a guarantee." Quillmere also boasted a flattering carbon footprint: far less water than field farming, short trips to stores and "green" power for its energy-hungry lamps. Nobody had checked those figures, and critics now speak of greenwashing. "Our shoppers pay more for those promises," says Rosalind Tchakarova-Webb, a produce 55 buyer for a grocery chain. "If the sales figures were wrong, why trust the carbon figures?" The Federal Trade Commission's Green Guides, last revised in 2012, warn against broad claims such as "eco-friendly." Yet checking such figures is costly, and methods to measure a farm's emissions are not yet settled. 60 Employees, many of them shareholders, fear for their savings and for local jobs. The restatement shows that controls can work in the end. But a clean opinion is not proof that nothing is wrong. Trust in audited accounts rests on prudent managers, on an internal audit team and an audit committee that do their jobs, and on an external auditor independent enough to say no. Green figures will need 65 the same discipline.

Question

To what extent can investors still trust a company's audited accounts?
