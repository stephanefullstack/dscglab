## Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

1. la **synthèse** du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
2. le **commentaire organisé** du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un **entretien en anglais** de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

*Le document est un article de presse fictif rédigé pour l'entraînement à partir de faits vérifiés ; la publication, le titre, l'auteur, l'entreprise et les personnes citées sont fictifs.*

**The Pennine Business Review** (fictitious publication, written for training) — 9 November 2026

#### No TV slot, fifty phones: a Leeds snack brand bets on influencers

*A young Yorkshire brand is launching a lower-sugar bar without a TV advert. Its founder praises influencers; a health researcher sees a loophole.*

*By Tamsin Whitlowe (fictitious author)*

Kit Ostler holds a small green bar up to her phone and takes a bite. "Crunchy, and  
it doesn't taste like a diet," she tells the camera. The word "Ad" is on screen from  
the first second. Ms Ostler, a fictitious student in Sheffield, is one of fifty  
micro-influencers paid to launch a new bar from Tarnwick Snacks.  
L05 Tarnwick, a fictitious brand founded in Leeds four years ago, employs about twenty  
people, is backed by private investors and has its products made by a contract  
manufacturer. Its new oat bar contains less sugar than most rival bars and is aimed  
at sixteen-to-twenty-four-year-olds. The positioning is simple: a treat with less  
sugar, not a health food. "We are not selling virtue," says Nadia Brennock, the  
L10 firm's fictitious co-founder.  
What Tarnwick is not doing is just as telling: no television advert, no billboard,  
no paid banner. For decades, a national product launch meant a TV campaign. "A  
single TV campaign would cost more than our whole launch, and our customers hardly  
watch live television," Ms Brennock argues. "They watch each other." To her,  
L15 influencer marketing is word of mouth on a large scale: micro-influencers have small  
but loyal audiences, and their videos feel authentic rather than staged.  
Television is also restricted. Since 5 January 2026 (the previous date, 1 October  
2025, was dropped), adverts for less healthy food and drink have been banned on UK  
television between 5.30am and 9pm, and paid-for adverts for them are banned online  
L20 at any time. The regulator treats a paid influencer post about such a product as  
paid-for online advertising. The law, however, does not apply to food and drink  
firms with fewer than 250 employees.  
Tarnwick believes its bar falls outside the restrictions and, as a small firm, would  
be exempt anyway. "We are not hiding behind an exemption," Ms Brennock insists.  
L25 Disclosure rules, by contrast, apply to everyone. In Britain, the Advertising  
Standards Authority (ASA) enforces the code written by the Committee of Advertising  
Practice (CAP). Their guide for influencers, updated in October 2026, requires a  
post to be labelled as advertising if the creator has received money, free products  
or another incentive. The label must be visible upfront: "#ad" works, while  
L30 "gifted", "collab" or even "sponsored" are not clear enough. Brand, influencer and  
agency are all responsible. In ASA research from February 2026, 79 per cent of  
people in the UK said that ad labels should be visible straight away.  
American rules are similar. Under the Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides,  
revised in 2023, a free product should be disclosed, and "#ad" at the start of a  
L35 post is likely to be effective. The FTC also warns that a label that works with  
adults may not work with younger people.  
The European Union goes further: under its Digital Services Act, online platforms  
may no longer show targeted adverts to children at all. Yet in a check published in  
2024 by the European Commission and national consumer authorities, 97 per cent of  
L40 576 influencers had posted commercial content, but only one in five systematically  
disclosed it as advertising.  
Ms Ostler is confident: "If I lied about a snack, my followers would know within an  
hour." Tarnwick requires "Ad" in the first seconds of every video and checks each  
post before it goes live.  
L45 Dr Leonie Pratt-Varghese, a fictitious public health researcher, is not reassured.  
"Young people are bad at recognising advertising when it looks like a friend's  
video, label or no label," she says. She also questions the exemption: "The law  
stops the giants from paying creators to push sugary snacks online. A small firm can  
do exactly that. Children's diets do not depend on the size of the company."  
L50 There is a business risk too: for a young firm, brand image is the main asset.  
Tarnwick owns no factory, so its value lies mostly in its name and its customers'  
trust. Fifty creators means fifty chances for something to go wrong: a joke that  
offends, a post that calls the bar "basically healthy", a missing label. One  
misleading video can travel faster than any advert, and years of brand equity can be  
L55 lost in a weekend.  
Dr Pratt-Varghese would like regulators to treat influencer content exactly like  
traditional advertising, whatever the size of the brand. Ms Brennock could live with  
that. "Trust is the one thing we can't afford to lose," she says. Whether fifty  
phones build a national brand or a backlash should be clear by the spring.  

### Question

**Should influencer marketing be regulated as strictly as traditional advertising?**
