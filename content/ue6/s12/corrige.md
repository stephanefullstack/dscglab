## 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

*Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres se regroupent en une idée au lieu d'être récités : le télétravail reste répandu aux États-Unis, surtout dans les métiers d'ingénierie (l. 18-21) ; le décret et l'EEOC forment un contexte juridique, pas une interdiction générale de la diversité (l. 23-31).*

**Presenting the document** — This article, entitled "Three clocks, one company? A Denver engineering firm calls its staff back", was published in *The Front Range Commerce Bulletin*, a (fictitious) Colorado business publication, on November 18, 2026. Written by Brynn Halloway-Starke, it uses the case of Talvenridge Engineering, a fictitious civil engineering firm based in Denver with design offices in Mexico and India, to ask whether people who rarely share an office, a country or a culture can work as one team.

**Main idea** — A new chief executive wants to "make us one company again" (l. 12) by bringing American staff back to the office four days a week, turning headquarters into a flex office and replacing the DEI program with a "merit-based" policy (l. 11-17). Colleagues in Monterrey and Pune fear they will become second-class members of the firm and ask for better management of distance and cultural differences (l. 37-53). The author concludes that a dispersed team can become one team if its managers learn to lead it (l. 63-64).

**Logical structure of the argument**
1. **One meeting, three clocks** — A design review starts at eight in the morning in Denver, while the Monterrey project manager has been at work for an hour and a Pune engineer joins after dinner; few of them have ever met (l. 1-4).
2. **A dispersed firm** — Talvenridge, owned by its partners, designs bridges, water and sewer networks and roads, mostly for public clients; it employs about eighteen hundred people, has bought design offices in Monterrey and Pune, and its teams span three time zones, with most members working remotely for part of the week (l. 5-10).
3. **A plan for "one company"** — The new chief executive, recruited from a large industrial group, will require American staff to come to the office four days a week, turn the Denver headquarters into a flex office and replace the DEI program with a "merit-based" policy: the same rules for everyone, no targets for any group (l. 11-17).
4. **A national context** — Remote work remains common in the US, especially in engineering: according to the BLS, 35 percent of employed Americans did some or all of their work at home on days worked in 2025; in October 2026, 22.1 percent of people at work teleworked, and 35.3 percent in architecture and engineering; yet the chief executive is not alone, as many large employers have issued return-to-office mandates (l. 18-22).
5. **A legal context for DEI** — A January 2025 executive order revoked a 1965 order on affirmative action by federal contractors and requires them to certify that they run no DEI programs that break federal anti-discrimination law; the EEOC warns that a DEI initiative may be unlawful if a protected trait motivates an employment decision; the administration and its critics disagree, and many of the firm's public clients spend federal money (l. 23-31).
6. **The chief executive's reasons** — Young engineers learn by sitting next to experienced colleagues, construction problems need a team that can meet within the hour, and everyone should be judged on the same criteria (l. 32-36).
7. **The view from Monterrey** — The foreign offices, which never had desks in Denver, fear becoming "second-tier" subcontractors; meetings are set at Denver time; direct public criticism, seen as efficient in Denver, is badly received by many of her engineers, though she refuses to generalize; the diversity program helped hire and keep rare specialists (l. 37-44).
8. **The view from Pune** — Isolation, silence misread as agreement, two departures; talent retention requires inclusion and training managers to listen, to adapt feedback and to develop cultural intelligence; better use of collaboration tools (written decisions, recorded meetings, rotating late calls) (l. 45-53).
9. **Three models** — Full office (common culture, help for beginners, but teams abroad left out and more car commutes), organized hybrid work (contact and time to focus), fully remote work with regular meetings (equal treatment of all offices, but it requires trust and management by results) (l. 54-59).
10. **A qualified conclusion** — Distance weakens informal learning, time zones are a real constraint, merit-based policies are not unfair by nature and DEI programs have critics; but "one company" will sound hollow abroad unless Denver changes its habits; managers who learn to lead can turn a dispersed team into one team (l. 60-64).

**Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05)** — "Having summarized the article, I will now turn to the question: can a company build one team out of people who rarely share the same office, country or culture? I will first show why distance and diversity really do threaten the unity of a team, then how managers can build one team across borders, before asking whether a common culture still needs a common place."

## 3. Commentaire : plans possibles

*Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le candidat gagne à distinguer les trois distances de la question (le bureau, le pays, la culture), qui n'appellent pas les mêmes réponses : un fuseau horaire ne se « gère » pas comme un malentendu culturel. Il évite deux pièges : réciter des « cultures nationales » comme des faits (le texte les nuance, l. 41-43) et juger la politique fédérale (JUR-T05, ton neutre).*

**Plan A — Yes, but one team is built by managers, not by buildings**
- Problematic: What does it take for distance and diversity to become a strength rather than a source of division?
- I. Real obstacles: informal learning and mentoring suffer at a distance (l. 32-35, 60); time zones and meetings set at headquarters' time (l. 1-4, 38-39); feedback and attitudes to hierarchy misread (l. 39-47); isolation and departures (l. 45-48).
- II. What builds one team: clear rules for everyone (fixed team days, rotating late calls, l. 53, 56); collaboration tools and written decisions (l. 52-53); trust and management by results (l. 58-59); trained managers with cultural intelligence (l. 49-51); inclusion as a retention tool (l. 43-44, 48-49).
- III. Limits: the office still matters for beginners and on construction sites (l. 32-36); a "merit-based" policy may also be a way to treat people equally (l. 61-62); not every job can be done at a distance.
- Conclusion: a company can build one team across offices, countries and cultures, but only if headquarters accepts that "one team" applies to it too.

**Plan B — Three distances, three answers**
- I. The office: return-to-office mandates, hybrid work and flex office; what presence brings (learning, speed) and what it costs (commutes, exclusion of teams abroad) (l. 11-14, 54-59).
- II. The country: time zones, acquired offices treated as subcontractors, international management of acquisitions (l. 5-10, 37-39); possible answers: rotation, asynchronous work, regular meetings in person.
- III. The culture: direct or indirect feedback, attitudes to hierarchy, the risk of stereotypes (l. 39-47, 49-51); diversity, equity and inclusion as a management issue and a legal issue, which differs from one country to another (l. 15-17, 23-31).

**Plan C — The company, the employees, society**
- I. For the company: a common culture and fast decisions against the risk of losing rare talent (l. 32-36, 43-48).
- II. For the employees: fairness, inclusion, isolation, the right to the same career chances whether one works in Denver, Monterrey or Pune (l. 37-49).
- III. For society: commuting and emissions (l. 56), the legal and political debate on DEI in the US compared with reporting duties in the UK and the EU (cf. exemples datés), public clients and their expectations (l. 30-31).

**Useful notions from the programme** — `5.4.a` cultural differences, direct / indirect feedback, high- and low-context communication, attitudes to hierarchy (power distance), stereotypes ; `5.4.b` international management, acquisitions abroad, time zones, offshore design centers ; `5.4.c` diversity, equity and inclusion, affirmative action, merit-based policy, equal treatment ; `5.2.c` telework, hybrid work, flex office (hoteling, US), return-to-office mandate ; `5.3.c` trust, management by results, isolation, presenteeism ; `5.1.b` listening, feedback, cultural intelligence ; `5.3.b` collaboration tools, asynchronous communication, internal communication (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

## 7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

- `JUR-T02` — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
- `JUR-E02` — synthèse linéaire qui récite les chiffres du BLS sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
- `JUR-E04` — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the CEO is wrong », « DEI programs are illegal », « remote work is the future »).
- `JUR-C03` — éluder la question : parler du télétravail en général, ou de la seule politique fédérale de DEI, sans répondre sur la possibilité de construire une seule équipe.
- `JUR-C04` — placage de cours : réciter des dimensions culturelles ou des théories de la motivation sans lien avec le cas.
- `JUR-T05` — technicité excessive : détailler le droit de la non-discrimination, le contentieux du décret, le droit du télétravail, ou chiffrer des économies de bureaux.
- `JUR-E08` — lecture de notes rédigées.
- `JUR-L06` — chiffres et dates mal dits (« thirty-five percents », « twenty-two comma one », « the twenty-one January »).
- Propre au sujet : croire que tous les salariés doivent revenir au bureau (seuls les salariés américains sont concernés, l. 13) ; confondre les 35 % de 2025 et les 22,1 % d'octobre 2026 (deux enquêtes, deux mesures, l. 18-21) ; dire que le décret interdit toute politique de diversité (il vise les programmes contraires au droit fédéral et la certification des contractants fédéraux, l. 23-26 ; l'EEOC écrit « may be unlawful », l. 27-28) ; présenter Talvenridge comme une société cotée (elle est détenue par ses associés, l. 5) ou comme un contractant fédéral (ce sont ses clients publics qui dépensent des fonds fédéraux, l. 30-31) ; parler des Mexicains ou des Indiens comme d'un bloc (la cheffe de projet refuse de généraliser, l. 41-43) ; traduire *equity* par « capitaux propres » ; nommer une entreprise réelle ou un client réel ; prendre Talvenridge ou *The Front Range Commerce Bulletin* pour des entités réelles.
