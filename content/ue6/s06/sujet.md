## Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

1. la **synthèse** du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
2. le **commentaire organisé** du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un **entretien en anglais** de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

#### Contract or trust? A Michigan hospital network meets its nurses' new union

*After years of short staffing, nurses at a nonprofit health system have voted to unionize. Would better management have been enough?*

*By Corinne Ashby-Mendez (fictitious author)*

After eleven years as an intensive care nurse at Kestrelmoor Health, a fictitious  
nonprofit network of three hospitals and several clinics in western Michigan,  
Danielle Okonkwo-Price was told once again to stay for a second shift. "I said no  
for the first time in my career," she recalls. "Then I went home and started  
L05 organizing."  
Last month, she and her colleagues voted to join a newly formed nurses' union. For  
Kestrelmoor, which employs about three thousand people, including just over nine  
hundred nurses, it is a first: management must now negotiate a first collective  
bargaining agreement.  
L10 For years, the network has been short of nurses. Gaps were filled with mandatory  
overtime, and those who stayed watched friends leave for agencies, clinics or other  
careers. Turnover fed burnout, and burnout fed turnover. "We were only asking for  
enough people to look after patients safely," says Ms. Okonkwo-Price.  
For the union's supporters, the answer is a written contract and a collective voice:  
L15 staffing levels guaranteed on paper, limits on forced overtime and a way to raise  
concerns without fear. "A good manager can listen to you," she says. "A contract  
means they have to." In their view, only an organized workforce can balance the  
power of an employer that controls schedules, budgets and careers.  
Graham Lindqvist, the network's fictitious chief nursing officer, does not doubt  
L20 that his nurses are exhausted. He doubts the remedy. Before the vote, part of the  
board backed another plan: self-scheduling, retention bonuses, training for  
frontline managers, and unit councils where nurses, not executives, decide how their  
ward is run. "Nobody becomes a nurse for the money," he argues. "People stay because  
the work means something and because they trust the people around them. Rigid rules  
L25 can damage that." Bonuses help, he concedes, but the leadership style of a unit  
manager matters more.  
He also questions who speaks for whom. "I was a bedside nurse for twenty years. I  
don't need a third party to tell me what nurses feel." Ms. Okonkwo-Price disagrees:  
"He used to be one of us. Now he answers to the board. Our representatives answer to  
L30 us."  
Nationally, the tide runs the other way. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics  
(BLS), 10.0 percent of American wage and salary workers belonged to a union in 2025,  
down from 20.1 percent in 1983. In the private sector, where nonprofit hospitals  
belong, the rate was just 5.9 percent, against 32.9 percent in the public sector.  
L35 Strikes, however, are back. The BLS counted 30 major work stoppages beginning in  
2025, involving 306,800 workers, far above the average of 17.8 a year over  
2006-2025. Education and health services accounted for 196,500 of those workers.  
Michigan itself has switched sides in a long national fight: in 2023, it repealed  
its right-to-work law for private employers. In states that keep such laws, each  
L40 worker covered by a union contract decides whether to join the union and pay dues,  
even though the contract protects everyone.  
The labor market gives nurses leverage. In September 2026, 3.4 million American  
workers quit their jobs, a quits rate of 2.1 percent. In January 2026, the median  
worker had been with the same employer for 4.1 years. Every experienced nurse who  
L45 leaves takes years of know-how out of the door.  
The debate is also about what an employer owes its staff. Kestrelmoor's wellness  
program, with free counseling and quiet rooms, was welcome, nurses say, but it  
treated the symptoms of burnout, not the cause: too much work for too few people.  
"Exhausted nurses make mistakes," says Ms. Okonkwo-Price. "Looking after us is part  
L50 of looking after patients."  
Dr. Helen Varga-Whitcombe, a fictitious labor relations researcher at the equally  
fictitious Brantwood State University, sees risks on both sides. A contract can  
protect staffing, she says, but a hostile relationship can freeze an organization  
when every change to a schedule becomes a negotiation. A strike in intensive care  
L55 also raises a question other strikes do not: who looks after the patients? Yet she  
warns managers: "Participative management lasts as long as the people at the top  
want it to. A union does not depend on goodwill."  
Mr. Lindqvist promises to bargain in good faith and to keep the unit councils. "Now  
we don't have to hope they listen," Ms. Okonkwo-Price says. Whether the first  
L60 contract becomes a truce or a battlefield will shape how Kestrelmoor treats, and  
keeps, the people at the bedside.
