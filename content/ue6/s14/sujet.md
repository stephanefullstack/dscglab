Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

1. la **synthèse** du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
2. le **commentaire organisé** du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un **entretien en anglais** de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

#### Slides before filings? A reactor-parts stock after the frenzy

*A Pennsylvania reactor-parts maker soared on the data-center boom, then crashed on its own annual report. Now a private equity fund wants to buy it.*

*By Corliss Ambergate-Rowe (fictitious author)*

In a former steel mill south of Pittsburgh, about four hundred workers make  
steel vessels, heat exchangers and modules for small modular reactors.  
Varnholt Reactor Works, the fictitious company that owns the mill, went public  
about eighteen months ago. It has no real revenue yet: its first delivery, to  
L05 an unnamed reactor developer, is still to come.  
This year, retail investors fell for Varnholt: from February to August, its  
share price rose about sevenfold. On brokerage apps and online forums, buyers  
repeated one story: data centers need huge amounts of electricity, and nuclear  
power can supply it with very little carbon. A Department of Energy report of  
L10 December 2024 estimated that data centers used about 4.4 percent of U.S.  
electricity in 2023 and could use 6.7 to 12 percent by 2028.  
Washington helps, too. In March 2025, the department offered $900 million to  
help deploy small modular reactors; in December 2025, it picked two teams for  
up to $800 million, with new power expected in the early 2030s. In June 2026,  
L15 its lending office made a conditional commitment of $17.5 billion in loans to  
buy long-lead parts for ten large reactors. Supporters call this green funding  
for low-carbon power; critics point to waste and safety, and note that a loan  
offer is not an order.  
Varnholt's managers rode the wave, with investor presentations almost every  
L20 month, guidance for a first delivery "in the spring" and a pledge to run the  
mill on carbon-free electricity within five years. None of this was required.  
Then, in late September, came the annual report on Form 10-K. In its risk  
factors and financial pages, Varnholt said the first delivery would slip by  
about a year, recalled its losses and warned that it would need new money next  
L25 year. The shares crashed, losing two-thirds of their value in three weeks.  
The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) sets the rules. Every year,  
listed companies file a 10-K, which describes the business, lists the main  
risk factors and includes audited financial statements, plus quarterly reports  
on Form 10-Q. Certain events must be disclosed on Form 8-K, usually within four  
L30 business days. Since 2000, Regulation Fair Disclosure has required a company  
that shares important news with analysts or some shareholders to make it  
public. The goal is a level playing field: the same facts for every investor.  
Climate is another matter. The SEC adopted climate disclosure rules on March  
6, 2024, then put them on hold during court challenges. In March 2025, it  
L35 voted to stop defending them; on May 29, 2026, it proposed to rescind them. No  
final decision had been announced by mid-November. Varnholt's climate pledge  
remains voluntary, and unverified.  
Renata Szczepanik-Hale, Varnholt's chief financial officer, denies that the  
company fed a bubble. "We filed everything on time and by the rules," she  
L40 says. "Our risk factors have warned for a year that the schedule could slip.  
The market read our slides, not our filings." The listing, she adds, paid for  
a new welding hall and kept the mill open.  
Trevor Koenigsmark, a thirty-four-year-old electrician from Johnstown, bought  
near the peak. "Every presentation said 'spring.' The delay was in the small  
L45 print of a document nobody reads on a phone," he says. "They kept the  
excitement going with announcements they did not have to make. The regulator  
protects big funds better than people like me."  
Last week, Corlenmoor Capital Partners, a fictitious private equity fund,  
offered to take Varnholt private at a price above today's but far below the  
L50 peak. "A company with no revenue and a ten-year cycle does not belong on the  
stock market, where every quarter is an exam," says Anjali Brennecke-Moss, a  
partner at the fund. "Private capital is more patient."  
Bubbles are as old as markets, and rarely have a single cause. They can also  
fund useful infrastructure, as railroads and fiber-optic networks did. More  
L55 rules would not stop enthusiasm, and would make listing harder for young  
companies. A take-private, and the delisting that follows, may hurt retail  
investors who sell at the bottom, or give them a way out. Private capital is  
also less transparent. And voluntary climate information has value, but only  
if someone checks it.  
L60 When a share price runs ahead of the business, the blame is shared. Companies  
should put their required filings before their story; investors should read  
before they buy; and the regulator should make sure that the facts are equal  
for all, and easy to read.
