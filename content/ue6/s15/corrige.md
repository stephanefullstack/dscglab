## 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

*Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres se regroupent en trois idées au lieu d'être récités : le commerce en ligne pèse près de trois livres sur dix du commerce de détail ; la taxe ne vise que les très grands groupes, mais son coût peut être répercuté ; défendre une création coûte souvent plus de 50 000 £.*

**Presenting the document** — This article, entitled "Found by whom? Bristol's makers weigh life among the giants", was published in *The Severn & Mendip Business Digest*, a (fictitious) business publication from the west of England, on 21 November 2026. Written by Freya Llewin-Cotter, it uses the case of a fictitious online marketplace for handmade goods owned by its makers, Thrumwell, to ask whether small sellers can stay independent when a few platforms control access to their customers.

**Main idea** — A platform cooperative of some three thousand makers, which owes its existence to e-commerce but finds most of its customers through a search engine, social media and app stores, has been hit by an algorithm change and by cheap copies of its members' work (l. 1-14). Its board must choose between staying on the platforms, leaving them or relying on the regulator; the chief executive, a copied ceramicist and a former marketplace executive give three different answers, before a compromise is put to the vote (l. 15-61).

**Logical structure of the argument**
1. **A marketplace owned by its makers** — Near Bristol's harbour, about sixty staff run Thrumwell, an online marketplace for ceramics, textiles and woodwork owned by some three thousand makers with one vote each; the board asks whether to stay on the big platforms, leave them or trust the regulator (l. 1-5).
2. **A model made by e-commerce, but dependent** — Online sales reached 29.2 per cent of retail sales in Great Britain in October 2026; yet most customers arrive through a search engine, social media adverts or an app downloaded from one of two app stores (l. 6-10).
3. **Two shocks** — An algorithm change pushed Thrumwell down the search rankings and cut sales by about a fifth; factory-made copies of members' work appeared, far cheaper, on a global marketplace (l. 11-14).
4. **The chief executive's diagnosis** — "A handful of firms decide who gets seen"; network effects make the biggest platforms gatekeepers between small firms and their customers; he wants to spread the risk across other channels and back tougher regulation (l. 15-20).
5. **Regulation is coming, slowly** — Since 1 January 2025, the CMA can give "strategic market status" to firms with substantial and entrenched market power; it did so in October 2025 for the leading search service and the two main mobile platforms, and in June 2026 gave the search firm six months to rank results fairly and warn businesses of significant changes; "by the time the rules bite", some makers may have closed (l. 21-28).
6. **A tax that may fall on sellers** — Since April 2020, very large groups have paid a 2 per cent digital services tax on UK revenues from search, social media and marketplaces; it raised around £800 million in 2024-25, but some groups passed part of the cost on to customers, which for a marketplace include its sellers; the tax is temporary, and the 15 per cent global minimum tax does not replace it (l. 29-36).
7. **The copied maker** — Design right and copyright protect her work automatically, and registration gives up to 25 years, but she would have to prove the copy and pay to fight it; most respondents to a government consultation put a typical design dispute above £50,000; ministers will explore a small claims route but will not make copying an unregistered design a crime; the platforms earn a fee on every copy (l. 37-45).
8. **The former executive's objection** — Platforms gave small makers a shop window on the world for the price of a commission; leaving them means losing customers; co-ops have small marketing budgets; many lookalikes are legal, and shoppers choose price and convenience (l. 46-51).
9. **Data and marketing** — Targeted advertising reaches interested buyers quickly, while a mailing list grows slowly; the ICO reminds small firms of the rules on consent, the "soft opt-in", unsubscribing and cookies (l. 52-56).
10. **A social and green case, and a compromise** — Fair prices keep skills alive and local goods travel less than imported copies; members will vote in January on staying on the platforms while capping each one at a third of sales; "we can stop handing them the only key to our shop" (l. 57-61).

**Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05)** — "Having summarised the article, I will now turn to the question: can small businesses thrive in a digital economy dominated by a handful of platforms? I will first explain why platforms are both an opportunity and a trap for small firms, then examine what regulation and law can and cannot do, before arguing that small businesses can thrive if they use the platforms without depending on them alone."

## 3. Commentaire : plans possibles

*Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le candidat gagne à distinguer trois leviers, ceux de l'entreprise (canaux, marque, données), ceux du droit (concurrence, dessins et modèles, droit d'auteur) et ceux de l'impôt, et à voir que la coopérative mutualise ce qu'un vendeur seul ne peut pas porter. Il évite tout chiffrage du coût d'une campagne, d'une commission ou de la taxe : le texte n'en donne pas.*

**Plan A — Yes, if dependence is managed rather than suffered**
- Problematic: Are small firms condemned to depend on a few platforms, or can they turn those platforms into one channel among others?
- I. Platforms as an opportunity: e-commerce opens distant markets (l. 6-7); a shop window on the world for the price of a commission (l. 47-48); targeted advertising reaches the right buyers (l. 52-53); the cheapest route to customers.
- II. Platforms as a trap: network effects and gatekeepers (l. 16-18); one algorithm change can cut sales by a fifth (l. 11-12); rules written by the platforms alone (l. 15-16); copies sold on the same marketplaces (l. 13-14, 44-45); a tax that may be passed on (l. 33-34).
- III. Conditions for thriving: several channels and a cap on each platform (l. 18-20, 59-60); protecting designs and building a direct, lawful relationship with customers (l. 38-40, 54-56); pooling resources in a cooperative; a regulator that guarantees fair ranking (l. 24-26).
- Conclusion: small businesses can thrive, but not alone and not on one platform; the cooperative is an answer to the size gap.

**Plan B — Stay, leave or trust the regulator: three strategies tested**
- I. Staying: low cost, reach, convenience for shoppers (l. 46-51); but loss of control over visibility, prices and data.
- II. Leaving: own website, newsletter, galleries, craft fairs (l. 18-20); a direct relationship and first-party data (l. 52-56); but small marketing budgets and slow growth (l. 49, 53-54).
- III. Trusting the regulator: strategic market status and fair ranking (l. 21-26); but regulation is slow (l. 27-28), the tax can be passed on (l. 33-34) and design disputes remain costly (l. 41-42); a complement, not a strategy.

**Plan C — Economic, legal, social and environmental**
- I. Economic: network effects and winner-takes-most markets (l. 16-18); price and convenience (l. 50-51); the cooperative as a way to share costs and bargaining power (l. 2-3).
- II. Legal and fiscal: competition law and the digital markets regime (l. 21-26); design right and copyright, and the limits of protection (l. 38-45, 49-50); a national tax waiting for a global deal (l. 29-36); data protection rules (l. 54-56).
- III. Social and environmental: makers' incomes and skills (l. 57); one member, one vote (l. 3); local production against long-distance copies (l. 57-59); but parcels sent to buyers far away also have a footprint.

**Useful notions from the programme** — `2.4.a` Big Tech, gatekeeper, network effects, winner-takes-all market ; `2.4.b` competition law, competition authority, strategic market status, conduct requirements, digital services tax, copyright, design right, infringement, counterfeit ; `2.2.a` e-commerce, marketplace, platform economy, platform cooperative ; `2.3.b` intellectual property, intangible assets, personal data, privacy, consent ; `7.3.c` digital marketing, search ranking, targeted advertising, cookies, mailing list ; `3.4.d` OECD, global minimum tax (15 %), taxing rights, profit shifting (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

## 7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

- `JUR-T02` — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
- `JUR-E02` — synthèse linéaire qui récite les seuils de la taxe et les dates de la CMA sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
- `JUR-E04` — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the platforms are monopolies », « the chief executive is right »).
- `JUR-C03` — éluder la question : décrire la Big Tech ou la régulation en général sans dire si les petites entreprises peuvent prospérer.
- `JUR-C04` — placage de cours : réciter les règlements européens ou les définitions du droit d'auteur sans lien avec le cas.
- `JUR-T05` — technicité excessive : détailler la procédure de désignation de la CMA, citer des articles de loi, chiffrer la taxe ou l'impôt minimum, entrer dans le droit des coopératives.
- `JUR-E08` — lecture de notes rédigées.
- `JUR-L06` — chiffres et dates mal dits (« twenty-nine point two percents », « five hundred millions pounds », « seven hundred fifty million of euros »).
- Propre au sujet : croire que la coopérative paie la taxe sur les services numériques (elle ne vise que les très grands groupes, l. 29-32) ; lire les 29,2 % comme la part des ventes de Thrumwell (c'est l'ensemble du commerce de détail britannique, l. 7-8) ; croire que la CMA a déjà sanctionné le moteur de recherche (elle a désigné des entreprises et imposé des exigences, l. 21-26) ; croire qu'une protection automatique dispense de prouver la copie (l. 38-41) ; confondre *counterfeit* et toute imitation (l. 49-50) ; affirmer que l'impôt minimum mondial remplace la taxe (l. 35-36) ; nommer un moteur de recherche, une place de marché ou un réseau social réels à la place de ceux du cas ; prendre Thrumwell ou *The Severn & Mendip Business Digest* pour des entités réelles.
