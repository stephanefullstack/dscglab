## 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

*Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les faits datés se regroupent selon leur fonction au lieu d'être récités : le chiffre de l'USDA situe le secteur (une niche) ; le rappel de la SEC dit pourquoi un retraitement inquiète : la direction répond des comptes, et les investisseurs doivent être informés ; les données du PCAOB nuancent le procès de l'auditeur (la qualité des audits progresse, surtout dans les grands cabinets) tout en montrant que les petits cabinets restent en retrait et que les estimations sont un terrain à risque ; les Green Guides de la FTC montrent qu'une allégation verte vague est déjà suspecte aux yeux d'un régulateur.*

**Presenting the document** — This article, entitled "Clean opinion, messy numbers: a Kentucky vertical farm rewrites its past", was published in *The Bluegrass & Ohio Valley Business Almanac*, a (fictitious) regional business publication, on November 26, 2026. Written by Corinna Albescu-Hart, it tells the story of Quillmere Greens, a fictitious Nasdaq-listed company that grows salad greens in vertical farms around Louisville, Kentucky, and has just announced that it must restate two years of accounts. It asks what a clean audit opinion is really worth, for financial figures and for green ones.

**Main idea** — The company's own new internal audit team, not its external auditor, found that sales had been booked too early and costs capitalized too generously (l. 10-21). For the author, the case shows both that controls can work in the end and that a clean opinion is not proof that nothing is wrong: trust depends on prudent managers, an internal audit team and an audit committee that do their jobs, and a truly independent external auditor, and green figures will need the same discipline (l. 61-65).

**Logical structure of the argument**
1. **The case** — Quillmere Greens, a listed vertical farming company with about nine hundred employees, has never made a profit but promised one for next year; it will restate its last two fiscal years, and its shares lost almost half their value in a day; indoor farming remains a niche, with about one billion dollars of food crops grown under cover sold in the United States in 2024 (l. 1-9).
2. **A discovery from the inside** — The errors were found by a new internal audit team set up at the audit committee's request, which reports to the committee rather than to the managers whose figures it checks (l. 10-13).
3. **Two errors, one direction** — Revenue was recorded in the wrong period: deliveries invoiced at the end of a quarter were partly returned, and discounts owed to grocers were recorded late; costs of starting a new farm and of work on the growing software were treated as assets rather than expenses, and equipment lives were stretched, which lowered depreciation; every choice made losses look smaller (l. 14-21).
4. **Why a restatement is a warning** — In 2022, SEC staff reminded companies that management is responsible for the financial statements and that investors must be told promptly when a serious error is found (l. 22-25).
5. **Honest estimates or missing prudence?** — The chief executive argues that a young company must make estimates, that his were too hopeful but honest, and that the company corrected them itself; a fund manager answers that accounts that change after the fact are useless for decisions and that prudence should have won; lenders ask for explanations and a supermarket chain suspends a contract (l. 26-33).
6. **What the audit did and did not do** — The mid-sized audit firm had given a clean opinion; the PCAOB reports that serious deficiencies have become much rarer at the Big Four (from 26 to 8 percent of inspected audits between 2023 and 2025) but still affect 32 percent of inspected audits at smaller U.S. firms, and it had warned that estimates are a high-risk area, open to management bias (l. 34-41); the internal auditor explains that she sees daily what an external auditor, testing samples a few weeks a year, finds only with hard questions (l. 42-44); a former auditor shares responsibility between management, the audit committee and the auditor, stresses professional skepticism and independence from a big client, and recalls that an audit gives reasonable assurance, not a guarantee (l. 45-50).
7. **Green figures under suspicion** — The company's flattering carbon footprint (less water, short transport, green power) was never checked; a supermarket buyer wonders why she should trust carbon figures if sales figures were wrong; federal guidance warns against vague claims like "eco-friendly"; but checking such figures costs money, and measurement methods are not settled (l. 51-59).
8. **Employees** — Many employees are also shareholders and fear for their savings and local jobs (l. 60).
9. **Conclusion** — Controls can work in the end, but a clean opinion is no guarantee; trust rests on a whole chain, and sustainability figures will need the same discipline (l. 61-65).

**Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05)** — "Having summarized the article, I will now turn to the question: to what extent can investors still trust a company's audited accounts? I will first explain why audited accounts remain the basis of trust, then look at what an audit cannot guarantee, before arguing that trust depends on a chain of people, from managers to auditors, a chain that must now extend to sustainability figures."

## 3. Commentaire : plans possibles

*Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06) ; les arguments du texte ne sont pas répétés sans analyse (JUR-C05). Le candidat gagne à distinguer « trust » au sens de confiance aveugle et au sens de confiance raisonnée : une certification donne une assurance raisonnable, pas une garantie (l. 49-50). Il peut aussi noter que la question dit « investors », alors que le texte montre d'autres utilisateurs des comptes (prêteurs, clients, salariés, l. 32-33, 60), et que le cas est ambigu : le retraitement est à la fois un échec (les erreurs ont passé deux audits) et une preuve que les contrôles finissent par fonctionner (l. 61). Il évite tout calcul, toute écriture et tout nom de norme.*

**Plan A — Trust, but verify**
- Problematic: Is a clean audit opinion still a reason to trust a company's accounts?
- I. Audited accounts remain the basis of trust. Users need them to decide (l. 30-33); management answers for them and must correct errors (l. 22-25); audit quality is improving, according to the PCAOB (l. 35-38); the restatement itself shows that controls work in the end (l. 10-13, 61).
- II. What an audit cannot guarantee. Estimates leave room for optimism and bias (l. 26-29, 39-41); the auditor tests samples a few weeks a year (l. 42-44); a mid-sized firm may depend on one big client (l. 48-49); smaller firms still have many deficient audits (l. 38-39); an audit gives reasonable assurance only (l. 49-50).
- III. Trust as a chain. Prudent managers (l. 32); an internal audit team and an audit committee that challenge (l. 11-12, 46-47); an independent, skeptical external auditor (l. 47-49, 64); the same discipline for green figures (l. 51-59, 64-65).
- Conclusion: investors can trust audited accounts to a large extent, but as a reasonable basis, not as a promise; their trust should depend on the quality of the whole chain, which they can partly judge (who audits, how independent the committee is, how prudent the estimates are).

**Plan B — Who is responsible for reliable accounts?**
- I. Management: estimates, optimism and the pressure of a promised profit (l. 4-5, 26-29); prudence versus optimism (l. 32); responsibility recalled by the SEC (l. 22-25).
- II. Inside controls: the audit committee and internal audit, which see the daily reality (l. 10-13, 42-44); their independence from management (l. 11-13).
- III. The external auditor and its regulator: clean opinion (l. 34); professional skepticism and independence (l. 47-49); inspections and their limits (l. 35-41).

**Plan C — Financial figures and green figures: the same trust?**
- I. Financial accounts: rules, audits and regulators, imperfect but real (l. 22-25, 34-41).
- II. Green figures: unverified claims, greenwashing risk, buyers and shoppers who pay for promises (l. 51-57).
- III. Toward the same discipline: assurance of sustainability information, international standard applicable from December 15, 2026 (cf. exemples datés), but costs and unsettled methods (l. 57-59); a proportionate approach for mid-sized companies.

**Useful notions from the programme** — `4.3.a` internal audit, external auditor, audit committee, independence, report to, sample, to challenge ; `4.1.b` prudence, accruals, revenue recognition, the right period, estimate, a fair picture (*present fairly*), going concern ; `4.1.c` users, investors, shareholders, lenders, customers, employees, decision-useful information ; `4.3.b` audit quality, inspection, deficiency, professional skepticism, management bias ; `4.3.d` restatement, regulator, Securities and Exchange Commission, Sarbanes-Oxley (2002), scandal, confidence ; `4.3.e` carbon footprint, green accounting, greenwashing, sustainability assurance, limited / reasonable assurance ; `4.2.b` tangible and intangible assets, to capitalize, to expense, useful life, depreciation, software development costs (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

## 7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

- `JUR-T02` — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
- `JUR-E02` — synthèse linéaire qui récite l'USDA, la SEC, le PCAOB et la FTC sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
- `JUR-E04` — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the chief executive is lying », « the auditor was incompetent »).
- `JUR-C03` — éluder la question : décrire l'audit ou les scandales comptables en général sans dire jusqu'où les investisseurs peuvent faire confiance aux comptes audités.
- `JUR-C04` — placage de cours : réciter les principes comptables, les étapes d'un audit ou l'histoire des Big Four sans lien avec le cas.
- `JUR-T05` — technicité excessive : passer des écritures de correction, chiffrer le retraitement, citer des normes comptables ou d'audit par leur numéro, détailler la méthodologie de l'audit, les procédures de la SEC, les recours des actionnaires ou le calcul d'un bilan carbone.
- `JUR-E08` — lecture de notes rédigées.
- `JUR-L06` — chiffres et dates mal dits (« twenty-six percents », « nine hundreds people », « one billion of dollars », « dollars one billion »).
- Propre au sujet : croire que l'auditeur externe a découvert les erreurs (c'est l'audit interne, l. 10-11) ; croire que l'audit interne dépend du directeur général (il rend compte au comité d'audit, l. 11-13) ; parler de fraude ou de mensonge (le texte n'en allègue aucune) ; croire que le taux de 32 % concerne le cabinet de Quillmere (c'est une moyenne des petits cabinets américains inspectés, l. 38-39) ou qu'il mesure la part des comptes faux ; prendre le milliard de dollars pour le chiffre d'affaires de Quillmere (ce sont les ventes de tout le secteur des cultures sous abri, l. 7-9) ; croire qu'inscrire un coût à l'actif est toujours interdit (c'est normal pour un actif qui servira plusieurs années ; le texte dit « too generously », l. 17-18) ; croire que la FTC a vérifié ou certifié des chiffres verts (ses guides mettent en garde contre les allégations vagues, l. 56-57) ; traduire *fiscal year* par « année fiscale » ; dire *auditor* à la française ; prononcer le *s* de *Louisville* ; nommer une société, une chaîne, un cabinet d'audit ou une personne réels, ou accuser une entreprise réelle pour une affaire récente ; prendre Quillmere Greens ou *The Bluegrass & Ohio Valley Business Almanac* pour des entités réelles.
