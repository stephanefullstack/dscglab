## 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

*Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres se regroupent en deux idées au lieu d'être récités : les règles et le permis poussent vers l'électrique, mais laissent du temps au diesel jusqu'en 2035 ; les Britanniques passent plus de vacances au pays de Galles, mais moins de séjours en camping ou en caravane.*

**Presenting the document** — This article, entitled "Old friends, new engine: a Welsh campervan maker reinvents its best-seller", was published in *The Tawe & Teifi Commerce Record*, a (fictitious) Welsh business publication, on 18 November 2026. Written by Elinor Prysedd, it uses the case of a fictitious family firm near Swansea, Gwennoral Coachcraft, which converts vans into campervans, to ask whether a heritage brand can change its product and the way it sells without losing the loyal customers and dealers who built it.

**Main idea** — Faced with the decline of its diesel flagship, an ageing owners' club and tighter rules, the firm launches an electric campervan, weekly rental and a subscription sold directly online to younger customers (l. 11-23). The chief executive sees innovation and the sale of an experience as the only way forward; a dealer feels bypassed and doubts the product; the marketing director wants to rethink the marketing mix and advertise for the first time, which some loyal owners see as a betrayal (l. 35-52).

**Logical structure of the argument**
1. **A brand built by its club** — Every August, hundreds of campervans fill a field in South Wales for the rally of Gwennoral's owners' club, mostly retired people; the rally has long been the firm's best advertisement (l. 1-4).
2. **The firm and its flagship** — A third-generation family firm of about three hundred staff fits out vans bought from large manufacturers; its flagship has been built on the same diesel van for some twenty years and is sold by about thirty independent dealers; word of mouth and loyalty replaced advertising (l. 5-10).
3. **A product in decline** — Falling sales, ageing buyers and tighter rules: under the plan confirmed in April 2025, new diesel vans may be sold until 2035, after which every new car and van must be zero emission; the best-seller has passed maturity (l. 11-15).
4. **The chief executive's answer** — An electric campervan designed in-house, weekly rental and a monthly subscription sold online to younger customers (servitisation); "you cannot save a product in decline with new cushions"; the brand sells freedom, the journey and craftsmanship, not metal and wood (l. 16-23).
5. **A favourable but mixed context** — Holiday trips to Wales by British residents rose by 20 per cent in 2025, to about 3.4 million, but trips based on camping, caravans or glamping fell by 9 per cent in Wales and 18 per cent in Great Britain; since June 2025, car licence holders may drive zero-emission vehicles of up to 4.25 tonnes, which leaves room for batteries (l. 24-30).
6. **A risky innovation** — For a firm of this size, the electric van is a disruptive innovation; it may bring a first-mover advantage, but followers spend less and learn from the leader's mistakes (l. 31-34).
7. **The dealer's objections** — Dealers built the brand, serviced the vans and took back the old ones; the firm moves from a push strategy through its dealers to a pull strategy aimed at end customers, which goes "over our heads"; range is limited and charging points are scarce in rural areas (l. 35-42).
8. **The marketing director's view** — Word of mouth only reaches people who already know the brand, and the club is not renewing itself; she is rethinking product, price, place and promotion and plans the first real campaign; some members see a betrayal of a brand that "never needed to shout"; she promises them priority and keeps part-exchange with dealers, and refuses to sell "a zero-emission holiday" (l. 43-52).
9. **Social and environmental stakes** — New skills in the workshop, with a promise to train rather than replace staff; an old diesel van kept running for thirty years is also sustainable (l. 53-55).
10. **A middle road, and an open ending** — A new version or new markets could extend the old model's maturity; the chief executive refuses: "a different way to reach" the same dream; the club will judge next August (l. 55-59).

**Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05)** — "Having summarised the article, I will now turn to the question: can a heritage brand reinvent itself without losing the customers who made it? I will first explain why reinvention has become unavoidable, then show what it puts at risk, before arguing that it can succeed if the brand changes its product and its channels but keeps its promise."

## 3. Commentaire : plans possibles

*Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le candidat gagne à distinguer ce qu'une marque change (produit, prix, distribution, communication) de ce qu'elle doit garder (la promesse : liberté, savoir-faire, confiance), et à voir que « les clients qui ont fait la marque » sont aussi les concessionnaires. Il évite tout chiffrage du prix d'un abonnement ou de la rentabilité d'un client : le texte n'en donne pas.*

**Plan A — Reinvention is unavoidable, but the promise must survive**
- Problematic: What must a heritage brand change, and what must it keep, to win new customers without losing the old ones?
- I. Why standing still is not an option: a product in decline (l. 11-15); an ageing club that no longer renews itself (l. 2, 44-45); rules that end new diesel vans in 2035 (l. 12-14); younger customers who may never own a van (l. 18-19).
- II. What reinvention puts at risk: older owners who may feel abandoned; a dealer network that loses its role (l. 35-40); the authenticity of a brand that "never needed to shout" (l. 47-49); a new technology with limited range (l. 41-42); subscriptions that may lower resale values and change the relationship.
- III. The conditions of success: keep the value proposition (freedom, craft, trust, l. 22-23); involve dealers in service and part-exchange (l. 49-50); give loyal owners priority (l. 49-50); avoid overclaiming on the environment (l. 50-52).
- Conclusion: yes, if the brand changes its product and its routes to market without changing its promise; reinvention fails when it treats the old customers as an obstacle.

**Plan B — Product, place, promotion: three tests for a heritage brand**
- I. Product: disruptive or incremental innovation? A first-mover advantage or the prudence of the follower (l. 31-34); extending maturity with a new version or new markets is a cheaper alternative (l. 55-56).
- II. Price and place: from ownership to use (rental, subscription, servitisation, l. 17-20); push through dealers or pull through the brand (l. 38-40); dealers as a source of service and trust.
- III. Promotion: zero marketing and word of mouth (l. 9-10, 48-49) against a first campaign (l. 46-47); the club as an asset that must be renewed, not replaced.

**Plan C — Economic, social, environmental**
- I. Economic: a declining product, new segments and new revenue from rental and subscriptions, against the cost and risk of a new technology for a mid-sized firm (l. 11-19, 31-34).
- II. Social: the skills of the workshop and the promise of training (l. 53-54); the future of thirty independent dealers (l. 8-9, 35-38); older customers and a new generation.
- III. Environmental: no exhaust on the road, but batteries with a footprint (l. 50-52); holidays closer to home (l. 24-27); a long-lasting diesel van as a form of sustainability (l. 54-55); charging points in rural areas (l. 41-42).

**Useful notions from the programme** — `7.1.a` goods and services, servitisation, product-as-a-service, rental, subscription ; `7.1.b` value proposition, customer needs, unique selling point ; `7.1.c` product life cycle, introduction, growth, maturity, decline ; `7.1.d` disruptive / incremental innovation, first-mover advantage, follower, product launch ; `7.2.a` marketing mix (product, price, place, promotion) ; `7.2.d` customer loyalty, owners' club, retention, push / pull strategy, dealer network, after-sales service, part-exchange ; `7.3.e` zero marketing, word of mouth, authenticity, brand heritage (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

## 7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

- `JUR-T02` — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
- `JUR-E02` — synthèse linéaire qui récite les chiffres du tourisme et du permis sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
- `JUR-E04` — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the chief executive is right », « electric vans are the future »).
- `JUR-C03` — éluder la question : décrire le marketing ou le véhicule électrique en général sans dire si la marque peut se réinventer sans perdre ses clients.
- `JUR-C04` — placage de cours : réciter les étapes du cycle de vie ou les 4P sans lien avec le cas.
- `JUR-T05` — technicité excessive : chiffrer le prix d'un abonnement, une marge ou une part de marché, valoriser la marque, détailler les contrats de concession ou les normes d'émissions.
- `JUR-E08` — lecture de notes rédigées.
- `JUR-L06` — chiffres et dates mal dits (« three point four millions », « twenty percents », « a three-and-a-half-tonnes limit »).
- Propre au sujet : croire que les fourgons diesel neufs sont interdits dès 2030 (les camionnettes thermiques neuves restent en vente jusqu'en 2035, l. 13-14) ; lire les 20 % comme une hausse de tous les voyages (il s'agit des voyages de vacances avec nuitée, l. 24-25) ; confondre *campervan*, *caravan* et *motorhome* ; lire 4,25 tonnes comme une limite pour toutes les camionnettes (elle vaut pour les véhicules à zéro émission conduits avec un permis voiture, l. 27-29) ; confondre stratégie *pull* et vente directe : le *pull* crée la demande du client final (club, bouche-à-oreille, campagne), qui achète ensuite chez un concessionnaire ou en ligne (l. 38-40) ; oublier que les concessionnaires sont aussi des « clients qui ont fait la marque » ; inventer des chiffres de ventes ou de prix ; nommer un constructeur, un aménageur ou un concessionnaire réel ; prendre Gwennoral Coachcraft ou *The Tawe & Teifi Commerce Record* pour des entités réelles.
