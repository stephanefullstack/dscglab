## 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

*Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres se regroupent en trois idées au lieu d'être récités : les envois de fonds pèsent des centaines de milliards de dollars et coûtent encore deux fois la cible des Nations unies ; les billets ne sont qu'une petite part d'une monnaie surtout créée par le crédit, sous le contrôle d'une banque centrale qui relève ses taux ; la monnaie du pays qui reçoit peut perdre beaucoup de sa valeur pendant que les prix y montent.*

**Presenting the document** — This article, entitled "Sending pounds home: whose money should a Leicester app trust?", was published in *The Soar & Welland Business Compass*, a (fictitious) business publication from the East Midlands, on 20 November 2026. Written by Imogen Ashworth-Lyle, it uses the case of a fictitious e-money firm, Tellanby, whose app lets people in Leicester send money to their families in India, Kenya and Nigeria, to ask which kind of money families and firms should trust: a private stablecoin, a public digital pound or bank money.

**Main idea** — Remittances are vast and still too expensive, and new forms of digital money promise cheaper, faster and more transparent transfers (l. 1-13, 25-29). But money rests on trust: most of it is created by banks under the control of the central bank, and a private token is only as safe as its reserves, which is why regulators are setting strict rules; for the families who receive the money, the exchange rate and local inflation matter more than the technology (l. 14-24, 35-57).

**Logical structure of the argument**
1. **A Leicester fintech at a crossroads** — Tellanby, a fictitious e-money firm of about a hundred and twenty staff, founded by two children of immigrants and backed by a venture capital investor, lets thousands of people send money to India, Kenya and Nigeria; its board must choose between a stablecoin, a public digital pound and the banks (l. 1-6).
2. **The stakes** — Low- and middle-income countries received about $704 billion in remittances in 2025, India some $151 billion, more than any other country; yet remittances remain costly, a barrier to financial inclusion: the United Nations wants costs below 3 per cent by 2030, while sending $200 still cost 6.36 per cent on average in the third quarter of 2025, according to the World Bank, which tracks remittance flows and prices (l. 7-13).
3. **The forms of money** — Only Bank of England notes and Royal Mint coins are legal tender in England and Wales, yet they make up only about 3 per cent of the money in the economy; most of the rest is bank deposits; customers' pounds become e-money in the app, which is not covered like bank deposits but must be kept separate under rules tightened in May 2026 (l. 14-19).
4. **Who creates money** — Commercial banks create most money when they lend; the central bank steers the money supply through its interest rate and raised Bank Rate to 4 per cent on 5 November 2026, as inflation reached 3.6 per cent and is expected to approach 5 per cent by mid-2027 (l. 20-24).
5. **The founder's case** — A stablecoin, issued by a private firm and backed one for one by safe assets in pounds, settled in minutes with charges shown upfront, would give families back what hidden exchange-rate mark-ups take from them (l. 25-29).
6. **Distrust and competition** — In November 2014, the FCA fined five banks £1.1 billion after traders tried to manipulate benchmark exchange rates; in July 2025, a US law set rules for fully backed dollar stablecoins, and the founder fears Britain could be too slow (l. 30-34).
7. **The central banker's objection** — "A stablecoin is only as safe as its reserves" (l. 36); if deposits moved to stablecoins, banks would lend less and the central bank would lose some grip on money and credit; hence the Bank of England's draft rules of June 2026: at least 30 per cent of reserves held at the central bank, the rest in short-term government debt, and a temporary cap of £40 billion per coin (l. 35-41).
8. **The public option** — The Bank and the Treasury are still designing a digital pound, a central bank digital currency, with a decision on next steps due in 2026 and no launch before the second half of the decade; critics fear for privacy and for banks, while the Bank promises it would not see how people spend and would avoid energy-hungry methods (l. 42-46).
9. **The view of those who receive** — For a nurse who sends money to Nigeria, the exchange rate matters more than the technology: the naira fell from about 426 to more than 1,500 to the dollar between 2022 and 2025, while prices rose by 33.2 per cent in 2024; a weak currency stretches her pounds, but local inflation erodes the gain, and a pound token must still be changed into naira; the Kenyan shilling, by contrast, has recovered part of its 2023 losses (l. 47-54).
10. **Transparency, and trust** — Some directors think showing the full cost and letting competition work would be enough; the founder concludes that people trust money "because someone stands behind it" (l. 55-57).

**Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05)** — "Having summarised the article, I will now turn to the question: should the money we use every day be issued by private companies, or remain in the hands of banks and central banks? I will first show what private digital money can offer, then explain why money remains a matter of public trust, before arguing that private innovation is welcome only under public rules."

## 3. Commentaire : plans possibles

*Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le meilleur levier de problématisation est dans le texte : les banques commerciales sont déjà des entreprises privées et créent l'essentiel de la monnaie (l. 20-22), mais sous le contrôle de la banque centrale, avec des dépôts garantis ; la vraie question est donc de savoir si de nouveaux émetteurs privés (jetons stables, monnaie électronique) peuvent inspirer la même confiance, et à quelles conditions. Le candidat évite tout calcul de marge, de conversion ou de masse monétaire et toute description technique des jetons : le texte n'en donne pas.*

**Plan A — Private innovation, public trust**
- Problematic: Can money be left to private innovators, or does trust in money require a public guarantor?
- I. What private digital money offers: speed and lower costs for remittances (l. 7-13, 28-29); transparency against hidden mark-ups (l. 27-28); a long history of distrust of currency dealers (l. 30-32); competition from dollar stablecoins (l. 32-34).
- II. Why money remains a public matter: legal tender and central bank money (l. 14-16); most money is created by banks under the central bank's control (l. 20-24); a stablecoin is only as safe as its reserves; deposit outflows would reduce lending (l. 35-38); e-money is not covered like deposits (l. 17-19).
- III. A shared system: private issuers under strict public rules (l. 39-41); a possible public digital pound as a safe anchor (l. 42-46); transparency of exchange-rate costs as a first step (l. 55-56).
- Conclusion: private firms can issue the money of everyday payments, but only if a central bank and regulators "stand behind it" (l. 57).

**Plan B — Three options for the families who send money home**
- I. The stablecoin: fast, cheap, transparent (l. 25-29); but reserves, credit and regulation (l. 35-41), and the exchange rate still applies at the other end (l. 52-53).
- II. The digital pound: public, safe, energy-light (l. 42-46); but slow, uncertain, privacy fears, a risk for banks (l. 44-45).
- III. Bank money and transparent fees: no new money needed, competition and disclosure of the full cost (l. 55-56); but banks' past rigging (l. 30-32) and the UN target still far away (l. 10-13).

**Plan C — Who wins, who loses?**
- I. Families: cheaper transfers and financial inclusion (l. 7-13); but the exchange rate and local inflation decide what money buys (l. 47-54).
- II. Banks and the economy: deposits finance loans to homebuyers and small firms (l. 20-22, 37-38); new forms of money could weaken this channel.
- III. Central banks and the state: control of money and inflation (l. 22-24), rules for issuers (l. 39-41), privacy and energy questions (l. 44-46); Britain's choice against the US model (l. 32-34).

**Useful notions from the programme** — `3.1.b` cash, banknotes and coins, legal tender, bank deposits, e-money, stablecoin, central bank digital currency, cryptocurrency (in principle) ; `3.1.c` money supply, money creation, lending, credit, Bank Rate, monetary policy, inflation ; `3.4.c` exchange rate, mark-up, benchmark rates, rigging, weak / strong currency, depreciation, appreciation ; `3.1.a` central bank, regulator, reserves, deposit protection ; `3.1.d` fintech, e-money institution, payment app, venture capital ; `3.4.d` World Bank, United Nations, Sustainable Development Goals, financial inclusion (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

## 7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

- `JUR-T02` — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
- `JUR-E02` — synthèse linéaire qui récite les montants, les taux et les dates sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
- `JUR-E04` — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« stablecoins are a bubble », « the founder is right »).
- `JUR-C03` — éluder la question : décrire les jetons stables ou les envois de fonds sans dire qui devrait émettre la monnaie de tous les jours.
- `JUR-C04` — placage de cours : réciter la définition des agrégats monétaires ou l'histoire de la monnaie sans lien avec le cas.
- `JUR-T05` — technicité excessive : expliquer le fonctionnement des chaînes de blocs, détailler les règles de réserves ou d'agrément, chiffrer une marge de change ou une conversion.
- `JUR-E08` — lecture de notes rédigées.
- `JUR-L06` — chiffres et dates mal dits (« six point thirty-six percents », « four hundred twenty-six nairas », « one point one billions pounds »).
- Propre au sujet : croire que la Banque d'Angleterre « imprime » l'essentiel de la monnaie (ce sont les banques commerciales, par le crédit, l. 20-22) ; confondre la monnaie électronique de l'application et un dépôt bancaire garanti (l. 17-19) ; croire qu'un jeton en livres supprime le change à l'arrivée (l. 52-53) ; lire les 6,36 % comme le prix de Tellanby (c'est la moyenne mondiale, l. 11-13) ; croire que les amendes de 2014 visaient des sociétés de transfert d'argent (elles visaient des banques et leurs activités de change, l. 30-31) ; croire que la livre numérique est décidée (l. 42-44) ; confondre Bank Rate et taux de change ; affirmer qu'une monnaie faible aide toujours les familles (l. 51-52) ; nommer des banques, des émetteurs de jetons stables ou des sociétés de transfert réels à la place de ceux du cas ; prendre Tellanby, Kenwick University ou *The Soar & Welland Business Compass* pour des entités réelles.
