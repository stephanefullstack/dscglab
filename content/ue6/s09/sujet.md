## Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

1. la **synthèse** du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
2. le **commentaire organisé** du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un **entretien en anglais** de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

#### One database, two workforces? An East Midlands landlord takes its tenants to the cloud

*A housing association swaps a dozen ageing systems for the cloud, an app and tablets. Tenants and unions fear being left behind.*

*By Rhiannon Ashworth-Bello (fictitious author)*

When a tenant near Nottingham reported a leaking boiler last winter, she  
explained the problem three times: to the call centre, the repairs team and the  
plumber at her door. "Nobody told me she had called twice, or that her son has  
asthma," says Callum Brierley, the fictitious plumber.  
L05 Brindleholme Housing, a fictitious non-profit housing association registered  
with the Regulator of Social Housing, employs some six hundred and fifty people  
and rents about eighteen thousand homes around Nottingham and Derby. Almost half  
of its staff work in the field. Until this year, its work lived in a dozen  
pieces of legacy software that did not talk to each other, plus spreadsheets  
L10 kept by hand.  
Tenants and the regulator want faster repairs. Large landlords in England must  
report tenant satisfaction measures; the latest, published on 5 November, show  
progress: 83 per cent of non-emergency repairs were completed on time in  
2025/26, up from 77 per cent two years earlier. Yet about one in six tenants  
L15 (16 per cent) are dissatisfied with their landlord's overall service.  
Brindleholme's answer is a single information system. Its chief executive, Priya  
Wendover, has signed a five-year contract for an enterprise resource planning  
(ERP) system and a housing management system, both rented in the cloud. Since  
September, tenants can report a repair and pay their rent through a tenant  
L20 portal or a mobile app. Field staff use tablets that show each home's history  
and update jobs in real time; reminders go out automatically. "Everyone works  
from the same database," Ms Wendover says. "Less typing, fewer lost messages,  
and repairs we can follow from the first call to the last visit." Office teams  
now work in a hybrid way, and the old server room is closing: a monthly  
L25 subscription replaces the servers it had to maintain. "Our IT costs are finally  
predictable. Every hour we save on paperwork is an hour for the tenants who need  
us most."  
Technology alone will not deliver that efficiency. An information system is more  
than software: it is also people, procedures and data. Wrong records will spread  
L30 faster in a new database. Large ERP projects often overrun their budgets and  
deadlines, because organisations underestimate training and new procedures.  
Brindleholme's launch has already slipped once.  
Maureen Hallsworth, the fictitious chair of the tenants' committee, likes the  
app but fears digital exclusion. "Many of our tenants are elderly, or can't  
L35 afford broadband," she says. "If the only way to reach your landlord is a  
screen, you stop being a person and become a ticket number." In February 2025, a  
government plan cited estimates that 1.6 million people in the UK live offline  
and that a quarter of the population have the lowest level of digital skills.  
Brindleholme promises to keep its phone line open; tenants want that in writing.  
L40 Dean Okafor-Pryce, a fictitious union organiser for the repair teams, has other  
worries: the tablets record where operatives are all day. "Managers call it  
safety and planning. My members feel watched." He also sees a divide: hybrid  
working suits office staff, but nobody can fix a boiler from a kitchen table. In  
early 2025, 28 per cent of working adults in Great Britain were hybrid workers,  
L45 but 41 per cent of those with a degree, against only 4 per cent of those with no  
qualifications.  
Security is a further risk: the new database, run by an outside provider, holds  
tenants' incomes, health conditions and notes on disability or domestic abuse.  
The government's Cyber Security Breaches Survey, published in April 2026, found  
L50 that 43 per cent of UK businesses and 28 per cent of charities had identified a  
breach or attack in the previous year, phishing being by far the most common.  
Yet only 15 per cent of businesses reviewed the cyber risks of their direct  
suppliers. The cloud shares the risk with the supplier, but the landlord stays  
responsible for its tenants' data: the Data (Use and Access) Act of June 2025  
L55 updated the rules, not that duty.  
Brindleholme follows a European trend: according to Eurostat, 52.7 per cent of  
businesses in the European Union paid for cloud computing services in 2025. Big  
data centres may use energy better than an old server room, though their demand  
for electricity keeps rising.  
L60 For Ms Wendover, digital tools should give staff more time with vulnerable  
tenants, not replace them. Ms Hallsworth's test is simpler: "When my neighbour  
rings, will a real person still answer?"
