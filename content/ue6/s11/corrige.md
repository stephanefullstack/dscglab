*Note pédagogique : le texte oppose deux lectures d'une même décision. Pour la directrice générale, rapprocher la production est une **stratégie de croissance** : des livraisons fiables, un service plus rapide, un savoir-faire protégé par des brevets et des clients publics attentifs à la fiabilité (l. 20-27, 58-60). ## 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

*Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres se regroupent en trois idées au lieu d'être récités : l'industrie britannique dépend des échanges et l'État laisse d'abord les entreprises gérer leurs chaînes ; les chaînes se réorganisent ailleurs aussi (États-Unis, Chine, Mexique) ; la Pologne coûte moins cher que la moyenne européenne, mais l'écart se réduit et la frontière ajoute des formalités.*

**Presenting the document** — This article, entitled "Closer to home, or just costlier? A Glasgow oven maker rewires its supply chain", was published in *The Glens & Firths Business Chronicle*, a (fictitious) Scottish business publication, on 16 November 2026. Written by Isla Fernbrae, it uses the case of a fictitious family firm near Glasgow, Drumlarrie Catering Engineering, which makes professional kitchen equipment, to ask whether bringing the production of components back home, buying a supplier and launching a new range is a growth strategy or a costly form of insurance.

**Main idea** — After supply disruptions delayed its deliveries, the firm first built up buffer stock, then decided to reshore the assembly of its circuit boards and motors, to buy its Polish steel supplier and to launch connected ovens (l. 14-27). Its chief executive presents this as a strategy for growth; an independent director sees an expensive insurance policy and prefers dual sourcing; Poland offers a middle way, and a union representative welcomes the jobs but asks for training and guarantees (l. 37-62).

**Logical structure of the argument**
1. **A revealing delay** — A hospital kitchen in the Highlands waited eleven weeks for an oven that was ready except for one circuit board stuck on a container ship (l. 1-5).
2. **The firm and its offshored components** — Drumlarrie, a family firm of four generations and some nine hundred staff, designs, assembles, tests and delivers its machines in Scotland, but its boards and motors come from Asia, often from a single supplier, and its stainless steel parts from Stalwiko in Poland (l. 6-13).
3. **Disruption and a first answer** — The pandemic, disrupted shipping and trade tensions stretched lead times from weeks to months, and the firm had little visibility beyond its direct suppliers; it gave up just-in-time for buffer stock, which "kept us going" but "did not cure the shortage" (l. 14-19).
4. **A three-part plan** — Reshoring the assembly of boards and motors within three years; buying Stalwiko (vertical integration); launching connected ovens developed through its own R&D, protected by patents and in-house software; public buyers are said to favour reliable deliveries and local origin (l. 20-27).
5. **A wider context** — The UK's 2024 strategy on critical imports found that 75 per cent of manufacturing trade depended on simultaneous imports and exports between 2018 and 2020, and left supply chains "first and foremost" to businesses; the 2025 industrial strategy, a ten-year plan, puts advanced manufacturing first among eight sectors; in the US, imports from China fell from $440 billion in 2024 to $309 billion in 2025, while imports from Mexico rose to $534 billion (l. 28-36).
6. **The critic's case** — The independent director points to higher costs in Scotland, contracts won on price, capital locked up in the purchase and in stock, and a shortage of technicians; owning the whole chain makes the firm "heavier and slower"; dual sourcing would be a cheaper route to resilience (l. 37-44).
7. **A third road: Poland** — Moving assembly to Poland would combine nearshoring and friendshoring; labour costs there were about €17 an hour in manufacturing in 2025, against €35 on average in the EU; but trade with the EU has meant customs paperwork since Brexit, and Polish labour costs are rising fast (l. 45-51).
8. **The social view** — The union representative welcomes the jobs coming home but wants training for the new lines and guarantees for Stalwiko's staff; automated lines may create fewer jobs than expected, and the chips themselves will still be made in Asia (l. 52-56).
9. **An open conclusion** — Shorter routes cut the carbon footprint of each oven; the chief executive sees organic and external growth based on reliability and service, her critics an insurance policy paid for in price competitiveness; the next hospital tender will tell (l. 57-62).

**Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05)** — "Having summarised the article, I will now turn to the question: is bringing production closer to home a strategy for growth, or an expensive insurance policy for manufacturers? I will first show how it can create growth, then why it often works as costly insurance, before arguing that the answer depends on which steps of production are brought home, and where."

## 3. Commentaire : plans possibles

*Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Le candidat doit voir que les deux termes de l'alternative ne s'excluent pas : une assurance peut devenir un avantage concurrentiel quand les clients paient pour la fiabilité. Il gagne à distinguer les étapes de la production (on ne rapatrie pas tout), les lieux (Écosse ou Pologne) et les outils de résilience moins coûteux (stocks, second fournisseur). Il évite tout chiffrage du surcoût : le texte n'en donne pas.*

**Plan A — Insurance first, growth if customers pay for it**
- Problematic: Under what conditions does the cost of shorter supply chains turn into a competitive advantage?
- I. An insurance against shocks: single-supplier risk and long lead times (l. 10-17); buffer stock as a first, costly answer (l. 17-19); governments asking firms to manage their own resilience (l. 28-32).
- II. A potential source of growth: reliability and faster service as selling points (l. 26-27, 59-60); control of know-how through R&D and patents (l. 23-26); securing supplies through vertical integration (l. 21-23); organic and external growth (l. 58-60).
- III. The premium is real: higher production costs and tenders won on price (l. 38-39); capital locked up and rigidity (l. 39-42); automation limiting the jobs created (l. 54-55); chips still made in Asia (l. 55-56).
- Conclusion: reshoring is a growth strategy only where reliability, quality and service matter more to customers than price; elsewhere, it is insurance, and cheaper policies exist.

**Plan B — Three options, three answers**
- I. Staying in Asia with buffer stock or dual sourcing: the cheapest resilience, but stock ties up cash and does not cure dependence (l. 17-19, 42-44).
- II. Nearshoring and friendshoring to Poland: lower labour costs, an EU member and a friendly partner, shorter routes, but customs paperwork since Brexit and rising labour costs (l. 45-51).
- III. Full reshoring in Scotland: control, quality and a "made in Scotland" argument that the chief executive believes will appeal to public buyers (l. 26-27), but the highest cost and a shortage of technicians (l. 20-27, 37-41).

**Plan C — Economic, social, environmental**
- I. Economic: price competitiveness against reliability and innovation; integration against flexibility (l. 37-44, 58-61).
- II. Social: jobs brought home, training for new lines, the fate of the acquired supplier's staff, automation (l. 52-55); the candidate can compare with what they know of other countries, without unsourced figures.
- III. Environmental: shorter routes and a smaller carbon footprint (l. 57-58), weighed against the energy used by new factories; long chains are also a climate risk.

**Useful notions from the programme** — `6.1.a` steps of production (design, sourcing, manufacturing, assembly, testing, delivery) ; `6.1.b` supply chain, supplier, single supplier, lead time, visibility, disruption, resilience, dual sourcing ; `6.1.d` offshoring, reshoring, nearshoring, friendshoring, trade tensions ; `6.2.c` just-in-time, just-in-case, buffer stock ; `6.3.a` R&D, patent, intellectual property, know-how ; `6.3.b` vertical integration, acquisition, diversification ; `6.3.c` competitiveness, organic and external growth, competitive advantage (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

## 7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

- `JUR-T02` — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
- `JUR-E02` — synthèse linéaire qui récite les chiffres britanniques, américains et européens sans dire à quoi ils servent dans l'argument.
- `JUR-E04` — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« reshoring is the right choice », « the director is right »).
- `JUR-C03` — éluder la question : décrire les chaînes d'approvisionnement en général sans trancher entre croissance et assurance.
- `JUR-C04` — placage de cours : réciter les définitions de l'offshoring et du nearshoring sans lien avec le cas.
- `JUR-T05` — technicité excessive : chiffrer le surcoût, dimensionner un stock de sécurité, évaluer le fournisseur racheté, détailler les règles d'origine ou le droit des brevets.
- `JUR-E08` — lecture de notes rédigées.
- `JUR-L06` — chiffres et dates mal dits (« four hundred forty billions dollars », « seventy-five percents », « a ten-years plan »).
- Propre au sujet : croire que tout sera produit en Écosse (les puces resteront en Asie, l. 55-56) ; confondre *nearshoring* et *friendshoring* (l. 46-47) ; lire les 75 % comme une part des importations dans la production (il s'agit du commerce manufacturier dépendant à la fois d'importations et d'exportations, l. 30-31) ; lire 17 € et 35 € comme des salaires (il s'agit du coût horaire du travail pour l'employeur, l. 48-49) ; présenter le rachat de Stalwiko comme une diversification (c'est une intégration verticale en amont ; la diversification est la gamme connectée) ; inventer des chiffres de coût ou de délai ; nommer un fabricant, un fournisseur ou un syndicat réel ; prendre Drumlarrie, Stalwiko ou *The Glens & Firths Business Chronicle* pour des entités réelles.
