## Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

1. la **synthèse** du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
2. le **commentaire organisé** du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un **entretien en anglais** de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

#### Closer to home, or just costlier? A Glasgow oven maker rewires its supply chain

*After years of late deliveries, a family firm wants to assemble its own circuit boards in Scotland and buy its Polish steel supplier. Its board is split over the bill.*

*By Isla Fernbrae (fictitious author)*

Last winter, a hospital kitchen in the Highlands waited eleven weeks for a new  
combi oven. The machine was ready in a factory near Glasgow except for one small  
part: a circuit board stuck on a container ship somewhere between Asia and  
Europe. "Nine hundred people, and one missing board," sighs Catriona Drumlarrie,  
L05 the fictitious chief executive of Drumlarrie Catering Engineering.  
The family firm, also fictitious, has made professional kitchen equipment for  
four generations: combi ovens, industrial dishwashers and cooking ranges for  
restaurants, hospitals and school canteens. Its engineers design every machine  
in Scotland, and its staff assemble, test and deliver them from there. The  
L10 components are another story. Like many manufacturers, the firm offshored them  
long ago to cut costs: its circuit boards and motors come from Asia, often from  
a single supplier, and its stainless steel parts from Stalwiko, a fictitious  
family business in western Poland.  
That model worked while goods moved smoothly. Then came the pandemic, disrupted  
L15 shipping routes and trade tensions. Lead times for some parts stretched from a  
few weeks to several months, and the firm had little visibility beyond its  
direct suppliers. Its first answer was to give up just-in-time and fill a second  
warehouse with buffer stock. "Just-in-case kept us going," Ms Drumlarrie says,  
"but it put money on shelves and did not cure the shortage."  
L20 Now the board wants to go further. Within three years, Drumlarrie plans to bring  
the assembly of its boards and motors back to Scotland. It also wants to buy  
Stalwiko, a step of vertical integration that would secure its steel, and to  
launch a range of connected ovens. That range is the fruit of its own R&D: the  
firm has filed patents on the design and writes the control software itself. "If  
L25 we make the brains of the oven at home, nobody can copy them or hold them  
hostage," Ms Drumlarrie argues. Public buyers, she adds, increasingly favour  
reliable deliveries and local origin.  
Britain, too, is rethinking where things are made. In January 2024, the  
government published its first strategy on critical imports and supply chains.  
L30 It noted that between 2018 and 2020, 75 per cent of the UK's manufacturing trade  
depended on simultaneous imports and exports, and that managing supply chains  
was "first and foremost" a task for businesses. Its industrial strategy of June  
2025, a ten-year plan, names eight sectors to drive growth, with advanced  
manufacturing first. Across the Atlantic, US imports of goods from China fell  
L35 from $440 billion in 2024 to $309 billion in 2025, while imports from nearby  
Mexico rose to $534 billion.  
Not everyone is convinced. Ruaridh Tennent-Oduya, a fictitious independent  
director, admires the ambition but fears the bill. Production costs more in  
Scotland, and public contracts are often won on price. Buying a supplier and  
L40 holding extra stock lock up capital that a medium-sized company needs for its  
new range. Skilled technicians are scarce. "Owning every link of the chain makes  
us safer, but also heavier and slower," he warns. In his view, dual sourcing,  
with a second supplier for each critical part, would buy resilience more  
cheaply.  
L45 Between full reshoring and staying in Asia lies a third road: Poland. Moving the  
assembly there would be nearshoring and friendshoring at once, since Poland is  
close, belongs to the European Union and is a friendly partner. Labour is  
cheaper too: in 2025, an hour of work in Polish manufacturing cost employers  
about €17, against €35 on average in the EU, Eurostat reports. But since Brexit,  
L50 every lorry from Poland to Glasgow faces customs paperwork, and Polish labour  
costs are rising fast.  
Moira Calderhead-Price, a fictitious union representative at the factory,  
welcomes the jobs coming home. She wants every worker trained for the new lines,  
and guarantees for Stalwiko's staff in Poland. Yet she knows the new lines will  
L55 be highly automated and may employ fewer people than the headlines promise. And  
the chips inside the boards will still be made in Asia.  
Shorter routes also mean fewer containers crossing oceans, and a smaller carbon  
footprint for each oven. For Ms Drumlarrie, bringing production closer is a  
strategy for growth, both organic and external: reliable deliveries, faster  
L60 service and better products. Her critics see an expensive insurance policy that  
weakens competitiveness on price. The next hospital tender may show who is  
right.  

### Question

**Is bringing production closer to home a strategy for growth, or an expensive insurance policy for manufacturers?**
