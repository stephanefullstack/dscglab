## 2. Synthèse : éléments attendus

*Note pédagogique : la synthèse dure environ 5 minutes ; elle suit la logique de l'autrice, pas l'ordre des paragraphes, et ne contient aucune opinion (JUR-E02, JUR-E04). Toute citation est signalée avec ses lignes (JUR-E03). Les chiffres de la CISAC servent le contexte (la valeur des droits musicaux à l'ère du streaming), ceux de l'OMPI la comparaison (le poids croissant des incorporels) ; les dates (2002, 2005, 2024) situent les règles : ils se disent avec leur fonction, sans être récités.*

**Presenting the document** — This article, entitled "The hits the balance sheet can't hear", was published in *The Tennessee Valley Commerce Herald*, a (fictitious) American business publication, on November 16, 2026. Written by Corinne Abernathy-Vale, it uses the case of a fictitious Nashville music publisher listed on the Nasdaq, Larchmoor Songs, to ask whether a balance sheet can still reflect the value of a company whose main assets are songs, a name and people.

**Main idea** — The article argues that a balance sheet that leaves out most of what a company built on ideas owns tells investors only half the story, and that it should be completed with information that can be checked (l. 65-67); it also gives full weight to the case for prudence (l. 34-41, 57-59).

**Logical structure of the argument**
1. **A hook: value starts in a room** — A songwriter writes in an office on Music Row; "You won't find this room on the balance sheet" (l. 1-3).
2. **The company** — Larchmoor is a publisher, not a record label: it makes no recordings but owns songs and collects royalties; about three hundred staff, some sixty thousand songs, half bought as catalogs, half written by its own writers (l. 4-9).
3. **The context** — Streaming has revived old songs: €14.06 billion collected for music creators worldwide in 2025, 45.6 percent of it from digital uses; buyers paid high prices for catalogs with borrowed money, and higher interest rates made those deals costlier; Larchmoor borrowed too (l. 10-15).
4. **The puzzle and the activist's demands** — The shares are worth about three times book value; an activist investor thinks the price rests on hopes and the debt is too high; it wants an end to debt-financed catalog purchases and a share buyback (l. 16-20).
5. **Management's case: hidden value** — The balance sheet shows purchased catalogs at cost, less amortization, plus goodwill, but not home-grown songs, the brand, the talent scouts or the ties with artists; few tangible assets; "Our accounts were built to count machines and stockrooms, not choruses"; market capitalization says more (l. 21-28).
6. **The rules confirm it** — Under US GAAP, a purchased intangible goes on the balance sheet, most home-grown ones are expensed; the standard-setter consulted in December 2024; in 2025, little support for recognizing home-grown intangibles, some for better disclosure (l. 29-33).
7. **The fund's case: prudence** — A balance sheet protects investors and lenders; what is not on it is "only a promise"; market values swing with moods and rates and may be a bubble; goodwill and its impairment may reveal overpayment; a buyback disciplines management (l. 34-41).
8. **A third voice: those who write the songs** — Nobody asks the songwriters; royalty statements are hard to understand; she wants the annual report to show how much writers are paid, and how (l. 42-46).
9. **An international comparison** — EU listed companies have used IFRS for their consolidated accounts since 2005 (regulation of 2002); the IFRS Foundation profiles 170 jurisdictions, most of which require its standards, but American listed companies keep US GAAP; the same problem under both sets of rules (home-grown brands never on the balance sheet, most home-grown songs off it); intangible investment passed $10 trillion in 2025 in 29 economies, growing more than three times faster than tangible investment since 2008 (l. 47-56).
10. **An expert's nuance** — Valuing home-grown songs and brands is subjective and open to manipulation; users want different things (lender, shareholder, songwriter); information already exists outside the balance sheet; "not a bigger balance sheet … but a better story around it" (l. 57-64).
11. **The author's conclusion** — Whatever the board decides, a balance sheet that leaves out most of a company's assets tells only half the story; the other half must be told "in figures someone can check" (l. 65-67).

**Transition to the commentary (to be said in English, JUR-E05)** — "Now that I have summarized the article, I will turn to the question: is a balance sheet still useful when a company's main assets are songs, brands and people? I will first show what a balance sheet still does well, then what it can no longer see, before suggesting how it could be completed rather than replaced."

## 3. Commentaire : plans possibles

*Note pédagogique : tout plan cohérent qui répond à la question, annonce ses axes et les tient est accepté (JUR-C01, JUR-C07). La valeur ajoutée par rapport à l'autrice (confirmer, nuancer, contredire, illustrer) est décisive (JUR-C06). Un candidat peut défendre que le bilan reste indispensable, à condition de traiter l'écart avec la valeur de marché ; il peut aussi relever la tension dans la position du fonds (une action jugée chère, mais un rachat d'actions demandé, l. 39-41).*

**Plan A — Still useful, no longer enough**
- Problematic: Should we change the balance sheet, or add to it?
- I. What a balance sheet still does well: reliable, verifiable figures; prudence protects investors and lenders from inflated values (l. 34-36); debt and cash matter when a company borrows to buy catalogs (l. 13-15, 19); comparability across companies.
- II. What it can no longer see: home-grown songs, the brand, talent and relationships (l. 23-25); few tangible assets (l. 25-26); a market value about three times book value (l. 16); intangibles now outweigh tangible investment in many economies (l. 53-56).
- III. Completing it: notes and management report, streaming figures, how long rights last (l. 61-63); disclosure favored by standard-setters on both sides of the Atlantic; non-financial reporting, including how creators are paid (l. 45-46).

**Plan B — Useful to whom? Three users, three answers**
- I. Lenders: very useful; they look at cash flows from catalogs and at debt (l. 60); a prudent balance sheet is their protection.
- II. Shareholders: less useful; they price the future (market capitalization, l. 27-28), but market value is volatile and may be a bubble (l. 36-37); buybacks and acquisitions are judged on value, not on book value (l. 16-20).
- III. Creators and employees: hardly useful; they need transparency on pay and on the share of value they create (l. 42-46); the social pillar of sustainability.

**Plan C — Book value or market value: which tells the truth?**
- I. Book value: verifiable but partial; history of what was paid (catalogs at cost, goodwill, l. 22-23); goodwill impairments show past overpayment (l. 37-39).
- II. Market value: complete but unstable; moods and interest rates (l. 36-37); the catalog boom made more expensive by higher rates (l. 13-14).
- III. A "true and fair view" needs both: figures plus narrative; international standards (IFRS, US GAAP) differ in detail but share the same limit (l. 51-53).

**Useful notions from the programme** — `4.2.a` goodwill, brand value, intellectual capital, impairment (principle), purchased vs home-grown intangibles ; `4.2.b` tangible and intangible assets, intangible economy ; `4.2.c` book value, market value, market capitalization, price-to-book (en mots), takeover premium ; `4.1.b` prudence, reliability, true and fair view (US *present fairly*), fair value ; `4.1.c` investors, lenders, employees, creators, analysts ; `4.1.a` IFRS, US GAAP, IASB, FASB, harmonization ; `3.2.a` listed share, Nasdaq, share buyback, activist investor ; durabilité : fair pay for creators, transparency, non-financial reporting (sans technicité excessive, JUR-T05).

## 7. Erreurs fréquentes à éviter (rapports du jury)

- `JUR-T02` — omettre la synthèse : « fortement pénalisés, quelles que soient leurs compétences de communication en anglais ».
- `JUR-E02` — synthèse linéaire qui juxtapose les voix (directrice financière, fonds, autrice-compositrice, professeure) sans montrer l'articulation (une énigme, deux lectures, des règles, une troisième voix, une comparaison, une nuance, une conclusion).
- `JUR-E04` — glisser une opinion dans la synthèse (« the fund is greedy », « the market is always right »).
- `JUR-C03` — éluder la question : raconter le cas de Larchmoor ou définir le goodwill sans discuter l'utilité du bilan.
- `JUR-C04` — placage de cours : réciter les critères de comptabilisation d'un incorporel ou les principes comptables au lieu de les utiliser au service d'un argument.
- `JUR-T05` — technicité excessive : numéros de normes, écritures, amortissements ou dépréciations chiffrés, méthodes d'évaluation (flux actualisés, multiples), mécanique des redevances.
- `JUR-E08` — lecture de notes rédigées.
- `JUR-L06` — chiffres et dates mal dits (« sixty thousands songs », « ten trillions dollars », « fourteen billions », « the sixteen November »).
- Propre au sujet : confondre éditeur musical et maison de disques (Larchmoor ne produit pas d'enregistrements, l. 6) ; croire que les chansons écrites par les auteurs maison figurent au bilan, ou qu'une marque créée en interne peut y figurer ; confondre goodwill et marque ; présenter le rapport de trois fois comme un calcul à refaire ; dire que toutes les sociétés européennes appliquent les IFRS à tous leurs comptes (seulement les comptes consolidés des sociétés cotées, extension possible par les États) ; dire que 170 pays imposent les IFRS (170 juridictions sont profilées, la plupart les imposent) ; dire que les États-Unis interdisent les IFRS (les émetteurs étrangers peuvent les utiliser) ; présenter la consultation du FASB comme une décision ; attribuer le chiffre de l'OMPI au monde entier (29 économies, environ 57 % du PIB mondial) ; prendre 45,6 % pour la part du streaming dans le chiffre d'affaires de Larchmoor ; confondre *billion* et « billion » ; prendre Larchmoor Songs, Halvergate Capital, Caney Fork State University, les personnes citées ou *The Tennessee Valley Commerce Herald* pour des entités réelles.
