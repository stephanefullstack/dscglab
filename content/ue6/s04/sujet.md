#### Neighbors, not bankers? A New Mexico bakery tests the limits of local finance

*As lenders grow cautious, a family bakery weighs what its credit union and its customers can offer.*

*By Lena Marchetti-Hollis (fictitious author)*

At dawn, the lights are already on at Horno Azul Bakery, a fictitious family  
business in a small town in northern New Mexico. Its owner, Paloma Arrieta, has  
a problem many shopkeepers would envy. Her bread sells out before noon, cafés  
nearby want daily deliveries, and an empty store across the valley is for rent.  
L05 To grow, she needs a bigger oven and a second shop. What she does not have is  
the money.  
Her timing could hardly be worse. In September, the Federal Reserve raised its key  
interest rate by a quarter point, to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent, to fight  
inflation. On October 28, it kept rates on hold, and two of its voting members  
L10 wanted another hike. Credit will not get cheaper any time soon.  
Small firms were already finding it hard to borrow. In a survey by the regional  
Federal Reserve Banks in late 2024, 37 percent of small employer firms had applied  
for a loan or a line of credit. Only 41 percent of these applicants received all the  
financing they sought; 36 percent got part of it, and 24 percent got nothing.  
L15 As rates rise, lenders look even harder at collateral and creditworthiness.  
Ms. Arrieta has four options, and none is perfect. The first is to wait and pay  
for the project out of retained earnings. Self-financing costs no interest and  
creates no debt, but it would take years, and the empty store will not stay  
empty. "Every year I spend saving is a year someone else could open there," she says.  
L20 The second option is her own lender, Red Mesa Community Credit Union, a  
fictitious not-for-profit cooperative owned by a few thousand members. Credit  
unions are no niche: in the first quarter of 2026, the country's 4,250 federally  
insured credit unions had 145.8 million members. Red Mesa's loan officers know  
the town, so they can judge a borrower on her record, not just on a credit score.  
L25 "A big bank sees a small file handled by a model in another state," says Tomás  
Villaseñor, its fictitious chief executive. "We see a member who has never missed  
a payment." Yet his cooperative lends its members' savings and cannot lend more  
than it collects. When rates go up, it must also pay savers more, which makes its  
loans more expensive.  
L30 The third option lands in her inbox every week: online lenders promising a  
decision within a day. These fintech firms are fast, but they usually charge more  
than a credit union, and borrowers have noticed. In the same survey, net  
satisfaction with online lenders fell from 15 percent to just 2 percent  
between 2023 and 2024.  
L35 The fourth option is the most unusual: asking the town itself. Under federal  
rules, a small company can raise up to $5 million a year from ordinary investors  
through an online platform registered with the Securities and Exchange  
Commission. Ms. Arrieta's crowdfunding campaign would be far smaller: customers  
would lend modest sums, repaid with interest over five years. Several regulars  
L40 have already said yes.  
For supporters, credit unions and the crowd are filling the gap left by big banks,  
and this is financial inclusion close to home. The World Bank counts  
1.3 billion adults worldwide who still lack access to financial services. In  
rural America the problem is less dramatic, but a firm with a good record and  
L45 little collateral can be underbanked too.  
Not everyone is convinced. "Community finance is good at small sums, but growth  
needs large ones," argues Nathaniel Brisbane-Coe, a fictitious economist at the  
equally fictitious Sandstone Policy Institute. Federal microloans, offered through  
nonprofit community lenders, go up to $50,000 but average about $13,000: enough  
L50 for a mixer, not for a shop. "Neighbors who lend to a bakery risk savings they  
cannot afford to lose. If the business fails, friendship is not collateral."  
And credit unions, he adds, are not protected from the rate hike: their savers  
want higher returns too.  
In France and much of the European Union, small firms depend even more on their  
L55 banks than their American counterparts do, so when banks turn cautious, they have fewer  
places to go.  
Ms. Arrieta will probably mix the options: a credit union loan for the oven,  
savings and her customers' money for the shop. "The banks taught me to wait," she  
says. "My town is teaching me to ask." Whether local money can pay for growth,  
L60 and not just for survival, is a question many small towns now face.  

### Question
