## Présentation

Vous disposez d'une heure de préparation. Vous présenterez ensuite, en anglais, un exposé de 15 minutes au maximum en deux phases :

1. la **synthèse** du document ci-dessous : présentez le document, dégagez l'idée directrice et l'articulation logique de l'argumentation de l'auteur, reformulez dans vos propres termes, sans commentaire personnel ; toute citation est signalée avec ses numéros de ligne ; les données chiffrées sont intégrées ;
2. le **commentaire organisé** du document à partir de la question posée : annoncez votre plan, envisagez la question sous plusieurs aspects, appuyez-vous sur vos connaissances, sur l'actualité et, le cas échéant, sur votre expérience professionnelle.

Annoncez clairement, en anglais, le passage de la synthèse au commentaire. L'exposé est suivi d'un **entretien en anglais** de 15 minutes au maximum avec la commission, portant sur le document, votre exposé et d'autres aspects de la question.

Ne lisez pas de notes rédigées, sauf éventuellement l'introduction, les transitions et la conclusion.

#### Two bosses, nobody listening? A pet food maker rethinks its grip on its plants

*Falsified safety records at a Polish plant led to a recall in fourteen countries. Now a Minnesota pet food maker must decide how much freedom its subsidiaries deserve.*

*By Solveig Brandtmeier-Oakes (fictitious author)*

Halverline Pet Nutrition, a fictitious company, sells dog and cat food in more  
than forty countries. Its headquarters are in Minneapolis and its shares trade  
in New York, but most of its nine thousand employees work far from Minnesota, in  
plants in Brazil, Poland and Thailand. Its supply chain, from fishing ports to  
L05 poultry farms, spans three continents. Like many multinationals, Halverline uses  
a matrix structure: each plant director reports both to a global brand director  
and to a regional head.  
Last spring, the quality manager of the Polish plant, Ewelina Kasprzycka-Lund,  
discovered misconduct: safety test records had been changed so that lines could  
L10 keep running and meet monthly targets. She warned the regional office twice,  
then resigned and sent her evidence to the board. In September, Halverline  
recalled several product lines in fourteen countries, including the United  
States. No illness had been confirmed by mid-November, but hundreds of tons of  
food were destroyed, retailers cleared their shelves and the share price fell  
L15 sharply. The reputational damage may outlast the recall.  
The internal investigation found a second failure. The social audits of some  
suppliers of fish meal and poultry were a formality: visits announced weeks  
ahead, workers interviewed in the manager's office, checklists signed in an  
hour. Some also claimed their fish came from audited, sustainable sources. Due  
L20 diligence at headquarters had stopped at the paperwork.  
Scandals have often shaped the rules. After the collapse of Enron in 2001,  
Congress passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in July 2002. It created the PCAOB to  
oversee the auditors of listed companies, asked audit committees to set up  
confidential channels for employees' concerns about accounting, and protected  
L25 employees who report fraud. Since a 2010 law, the Securities and Exchange  
Commission (SEC) has also rewarded whistleblowers whose tips lead to sanctions  
of more than $1 million, with 10 to 30 percent of the money collected. In fiscal  
2025, it received about 27,000 tips and awarded more than $60 million to 48  
people.  
L30 The Food Safety Modernization Act, signed in January 2011, shifted the focus of  
the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) from reacting to problems to preventing  
them, for animal as well as human food, and gave it the power to order a recall.  
In Europe, a 2019 directive requires member states, including Poland, to protect  
people who report breaches of EU food and feed safety rules from dismissal or  
L35 demotion.  
Corinne Haldane-Voss, Halverline's chief executive, blames the organization  
chart. "The matrix gave every plant director two bosses, and nobody owned  
quality," she says. "Both bosses asked for volume." Her plan: plant quality  
managers will report directly to headquarters, outside firms will audit  
L40 suppliers without warning, and three hundred local managers will be trained in  
ethics and in how to raise a problem. "Our compliance hotline was built for  
accounting fraud," she admits. "Nobody thought of the labs."  
Ms. Kasprzycka-Lund is not convinced. "People in our plant did speak up. The  
warnings went up the chain of command for months, and the answer was always the  
L45 same: hit the numbers, fix it later," she says. "You can redraw the boxes, but  
if bonuses depend on tons shipped, people will cut corners. And no training  
helps if listening costs a manager a bonus. The problem is the culture and the  
targets."  
Teodoro Vasconcelos-Brink, an independent director, fears a different mistake.  
L50 "If every decision has to go through Minneapolis, many time zones away, the  
plants will slow down," he warns. "Group rules written in the home country  
cannot foresee everything. Our teams in each host country know their customers,  
their suppliers and their regulators. Treat them as suspects, and the best ones  
will leave." Decentralization, he argues, is not the cause of the cover-up.  
L55 Each side has a point. Recentralizing would reassure investors and regulators,  
for a while, but it would cost speed and local knowledge. Supplier audits can  
stay formal whoever pays for them, and ethics training can become one more box  
to tick. Protection for whistleblowers still depends on the country, and  
enforcement priorities change with politics.  
L60 A multinational can neither control everything from headquarters nor delegate  
everything to its subsidiaries. The shape of the chart matters less than clear  
accountability, realistic targets, independent checks, and local managers who  
are trained to bring bad news up, and rewarded for it.  

### Question
